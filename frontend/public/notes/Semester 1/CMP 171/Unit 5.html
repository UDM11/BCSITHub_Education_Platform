Unit 5: Data Communication and Computer Network

Transmission media · network types · topologies · protocols · hardware

5.1 Introduction

Data communication is the exchange of digital data between two or more devices via a transmission medium. A computer network is an interconnected collection of devices that communicate and share resources. Together, they form the foundation of modern connectivity — from local home networks to the global Internet.

📡 Purpose
Enable reliable and efficient exchange of information, resource sharing (printers, files), and communication (email, VoIP, video conferencing).
🧩 Components
Devices (computers, routers, switches), transmission media (wired/wireless), and protocols (TCP/IP, HTTP, FTP).
  • Data Communication System: Requires sender, receiver, transmission medium, and a protocol to govern the exchange.
  • Network Types: Range from small personal networks (PAN) to global networks (WAN).
  • Examples: The Internet, corporate intranets, home Wi-Fi, and cellular networks.
💡 Core idea: Data communication and computer networks enable the digital world — connecting people, devices, and information across the globe.

5.2 Communication Media

Communication media (or transmission media) are the physical or wireless channels through which data signals travel between devices. The choice of media affects speed, reliability, and cost.

Wired (Guided) Media

  • Twisted Pair Cable: Two insulated copper wires twisted together. Used in Ethernet networks (Cat5e, Cat6). Affordable but susceptible to electromagnetic interference.
  • Coaxial Cable: Central conductor surrounded by insulation and shielding. Used in cable TV and older Ethernet (10Base2). Higher bandwidth than twisted pair.
  • Fiber Optic Cable: Uses light pulses through glass or plastic fibers. Offers extremely high bandwidth, long distance, immunity to interference. Used in backbone networks (Internet, telecom).

Wireless (Unguided) Media

  • Radio Waves: Used in Wi-Fi (802.11), Bluetooth, and cellular networks. Omnidirectional, can penetrate walls.
  • Microwaves: Point-to-point communication (satellite, terrestrial). Requires line-of-sight. Used in backhaul links.
  • Infrared: Short-range, line-of-sight communication (TV remotes, IRDA).

5.3 Communication Modes

Communication mode defines the direction of data flow between two devices. It determines how devices exchange information.

Simplex Mode

  • Direction: One-way only. Data flows from sender to receiver.
  • Characteristics: Simple, no need for two-way communication.
  • Examples: Radio broadcasting, television transmission, keyboard to computer (unidirectional).

Half-Duplex Mode

  • Direction: Two-way, but not simultaneously. Devices take turns transmitting.
  • Characteristics: Efficient for applications that don't require simultaneous transmission.
  • Examples: Walkie-talkies, CB radios, Ethernet (traditional shared media).

Full-Duplex Mode

  • Direction: Two-way simultaneous. Both devices can send and receive at the same time.
  • Characteristics: Most efficient, but requires separate channels or advanced technology.
  • Examples: Telephone conversations, modern Ethernet (switched), fiber optic links.

5.4 Computer Network

A computer network is a system of interconnected computing devices that communicate and share resources. Networks range from small home networks to the global Internet.

Purpose of Computer Networks

  • Resource Sharing: Share printers, storage, files, and applications across multiple users.
  • Communication: Enable email, instant messaging, VoIP, video conferencing.
  • Centralized Management: Manage data, security, and policies from a central location.
  • Data Access: Access information and databases from anywhere.
  • Cost Efficiency: Reduce hardware costs by sharing expensive resources.

Components of a Network

  • Nodes: Devices connected to the network (computers, printers, servers).
  • Links: Connections between nodes (wired or wireless).
  • Network Devices: Routers, switches, hubs, access points.
  • Protocols: Rules governing communication (TCP/IP, HTTP, FTP).

5.5 Advantages and Disadvantages of Computer Networks

Advantages

  • Resource Sharing: Multiple users can share printers, storage devices, and internet connections.
  • Cost Savings: Reduce hardware costs by sharing peripherals and software licenses.
  • Communication: Enable instant messaging, email, video calls, and collaboration tools.
  • Centralized Data: Store data centrally for easy backup, management, and access.
  • Accessibility: Access resources from anywhere via remote connections.
  • Scalability: Networks can grow by adding new devices with minimal disruption.

Disadvantages

  • Security Risks: Networks are vulnerable to hacking, malware, and data breaches.
  • Cost: Initial setup (hardware, cabling, software) can be expensive.
  • Maintenance: Requires skilled staff to manage, monitor, and troubleshoot.
  • Dependency: If the network fails, all connected devices may lose access.
  • Complexity: Designing and managing large networks is technically challenging.
⚠️ Balance: The benefits of networking often outweigh the disadvantages, but security and reliability must be prioritized.

5.6 Types of Networks

Networks are classified by their geographic scope and purpose.

PAN (Personal Area Network)

  • Range: Very short, typically within a few meters.
  • Purpose: Connect personal devices (smartphone, laptop, headphones).
  • Examples: Bluetooth, Zigbee, USB connections.

LAN (Local Area Network)

  • Range: Building or campus (up to a few kilometers).
  • Purpose: Connect devices within a limited area for resource sharing.
  • Examples: Office network, home Wi-Fi, school computer lab.
  • Technology: Ethernet, Wi-Fi.

MAN (Metropolitan Area Network)

  • Range: City or metropolitan area (10-100 km).
  • Purpose: Connect multiple LANs within a city.
  • Examples: Cable TV networks, city-wide fiber networks.
  • Technology: Fiber optics, microwave.

WAN (Wide Area Network)

  • Range: Country, continent, or global.
  • Purpose: Connect geographically dispersed networks.
  • Examples: The Internet, corporate global networks.
  • Technology: Leased lines, satellite, submarine cables.

5.7 Network Topology

Network topology defines the physical or logical arrangement of devices in a network. The choice of topology affects performance, cost, and reliability.

Bus Topology

  • Structure: All devices connect to a single central cable (bus).
  • Advantages: Simple, inexpensive, uses less cable.
  • Disadvantages: Single point of failure, limited scalability, collisions.
  • Use: Legacy Ethernet (10Base2).

Star Topology

  • Structure: All devices connect to a central hub or switch.
  • Advantages: Easy to install, manage, and troubleshoot. Failure of one device doesn't affect others.
  • Disadvantages: Hub/switch is a single point of failure.
  • Use: Most modern LANs (Ethernet).

Ring Topology

  • Structure: Devices form a closed loop; data travels in one direction.
  • Advantages: No collisions, simple token-based access.
  • Disadvantages: Failure of one device can break the entire ring.
  • Use: Token Ring, FDDI (legacy).

Mesh Topology

  • Structure: Devices are interconnected with multiple paths.
  • Advantages: High redundancy and reliability.
  • Disadvantages: Expensive, complex, requires many connections.
  • Use: Internet backbone, military networks.

5.8 Communication Protocols (IP, TCP)

Communication protocols are sets of rules that govern how data is transmitted, formatted, and received in a network. They ensure interoperability between different devices and systems.

TCP/IP Model

  • Application Layer: HTTP, FTP, SMTP, DNS — user-facing protocols.
  • Transport Layer: TCP (reliable, connection-oriented) and UDP (fast, connectionless).
  • Internet Layer: IP (Internet Protocol) — addressing and routing packets.
  • Network Access Layer: Ethernet, Wi-Fi — physical transmission.

IP (Internet Protocol)

  • Purpose: Assigns unique addresses (IPv4 or IPv6) to devices and routes data packets across networks.
  • IPv4: 32-bit addresses (e.g., 192.168.1.1) — limited to ~4.3 billion addresses.
  • IPv6: 128-bit addresses (e.g., 2001:0db8::1) — virtually unlimited addresses.

TCP (Transmission Control Protocol)

  • Purpose: Ensures reliable, ordered, and error-checked delivery of data between applications.
  • Features: Connection establishment (3-way handshake), flow control, retransmission of lost packets.
  • Use: Web browsing (HTTP), email (SMTP), file transfer (FTP).

Other Important Protocols

  • UDP: Fast, no connection overhead — used in streaming, gaming, DNS.
  • HTTP/HTTPS: Web communication.
  • FTP: File transfer.
  • SMTP/POP3/IMAP: Email.
  • DNS: Domain name resolution.
# TCP 3-way handshake
Client → Server: SYN
Server → Client: SYN-ACK
Client → Server: ACK
# Data transfer begins
🌐 Key insight: TCP/IP is the foundational protocol suite of the Internet. IP handles addressing and routing; TCP ensures reliable delivery.

5.9 Networking Hardware

Networking hardware comprises the physical devices that enable connectivity, data transfer, and network management.

Router

  • Purpose: Connects different networks (e.g., LAN to WAN) and routes data packets based on IP addresses.
  • Features: Routing tables, NAT (Network Address Translation), firewall capabilities.
  • Examples: Home Wi-Fi routers, enterprise core routers.

Switch

  • Purpose: Connects devices within a LAN. Forwards data based on MAC addresses.
  • Features: Low latency, multiple ports, VLAN support.
  • Examples: Managed and unmanaged switches for office networks.

Access Point (AP)

  • Purpose: Enables wireless devices to connect to a wired network.
  • Features: Wi-Fi standards (802.11ac/ax), multiple SSIDs, security (WPA3).
  • Examples: Wireless routers in AP mode, enterprise APs.

Network Interface Card (NIC)

  • Purpose: Provides physical connection to the network (wired or wireless).
  • Features: MAC address, supports specific speeds (1 Gbps, 10 Gbps).
  • Examples: Ethernet NIC, Wi-Fi adapter.

Other Hardware

  • Modem: Modulates/demodulates signals for internet access (cable/DSL).
  • Bridge: Connects two network segments.
  • Gateway: Connects networks with different protocols.
  • Firewall: Filters traffic for security.

5.10 OSI Reference Model

The OSI (Open Systems Interconnection) model is a conceptual framework that standardizes network communication into seven layers. It helps in understanding and troubleshooting network operations.

The Seven Layers

  • Layer 7 – Application: User interface (HTTP, FTP, SMTP).
  • Layer 6 – Presentation: Data formatting, encryption (SSL/TLS).
  • Layer 5 – Session: Connection management.
  • Layer 4 – Transport: End-to-end communication (TCP, UDP).
  • Layer 3 – Network: Routing and addressing (IP).
  • Layer 2 – Data Link: Error-free transmission (Ethernet, MAC).
  • Layer 1 – Physical: Raw bit transmission (cables, radio).

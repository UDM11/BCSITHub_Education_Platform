Unit 1: Introduction to Data Communication [5 Hrs.]

1.1 Introduction and Fundamentals of Data Communication

What is Data Communication?

Data communication refers to the exchange of data (in the form of 0s and 1s) between two devices via some form of transmission medium, such as a wire cable or wireless channel. It is the movement of encoded information from one point to another by means of electrical, optical, or electromagnetic signals.

Fundamental Characteristics of Data Communication

The effectiveness of a data communication system depends on four fundamental characteristics:

  1. Delivery: The system must deliver data to the correct destination. Data must be received by the intended device or user and only by that device or user.
  2. Accuracy: The system must deliver the data accurately. Data that have been altered in transmission and left uncorrected are unusable.
  3. Timeliness: The system must deliver data in a timely manner. Data delivered late are useless. In the case of video and audio, timely delivery means delivering data as they are produced, in the same order that they are produced, and without significant delay. This is called real-time transmission.
  4. Jitter: Jitter refers to the variation in the packet arrival time. It is the uneven delay in the delivery of audio or video packets. For example, if some packets of a video are delayed by 30ms and others by 40ms, the quality of the video will be uneven.

  1. Message: The information (data) to be communicated. Popular forms of information include text, numbers, pictures, audio, and video.
  2. Sender: The device that sends the data message. It can be a computer, workstation, telephone handset, video camera, and so on.
  3. Receiver: The device that receives the message. It can be a computer, workstation, telephone handset, television, and so on.
  4. Transmission Medium: The physical path by which a message travels from sender to receiver. It can be guided (wired) like twisted-pair wire, coaxial cable, or fiber-optic cable, or unguided (wireless) like radio waves or microwaves.
  5. Protocol: A set of rules that govern data communication. It represents an agreement between the communicating devices. Without a protocol, two devices may be connected but not communicating, just as a person speaking French cannot understand a person speaking Nepali.

1.2 Data Transmission Modes

The transmission mode defines the direction of signal flow between two connected devices. There are three basic modes:

1. Simplex Mode

In simplex mode, the communication is unidirectional, like a one-way street. Only one of the two devices on a link can transmit; the other can only receive.

  • Direction of Flow: Sender to Receiver only.
  • Channel Capacity: The entire capacity of the channel is used by the transmitter.
  • Examples: Keyboard (input only), Monitor (output only), Television broadcasting, Radio.
  ┌──────────────┐   Unidirectional (One-way)    ┌──────────────┐
  │  Transmitter │ ────────────────────────────→ │   Receiver   │
  │  (e.g., Keyboard)                            │  (e.g., CPU) │
  └──────────────┘                               └──────────────┘

2. Half-Duplex Mode

In half-duplex mode, each station can both transmit and receive, but not at the same time. When one device is sending, the other can only receive, and vice versa. It is like a one-lane bridge where traffic can go in both directions, but only one direction at a time.

  • Direction of Flow: Bidirectional, but only one direction at a time.
  • Channel Capacity: The full capacity of the channel is used by whichever device is sending at that moment.
  • Examples: Walkie-talkies, legacy Coaxial-Ethernet.
  Direction 1:   ┌──────────────┐ ───────────────→ ┌──────────────┐
                 │   Device A   │                  │   Device B   │
                 └──────────────┘                  └──────────────┘
  Direction 2:   ┌──────────────┐ ←─────────────── ┌──────────────┐
                 │   Device A   │                  │   Device B   │
                 └──────────────┘                  └──────────────┘

3. Full-Duplex Mode

In full-duplex mode (often called simply duplex), both stations can transmit and receive simultaneously. It is like a two-way street where traffic flows in both directions at the same time.

  • Direction of Flow: Bidirectional, simultaneously.
  • Channel Capacity: The capacity of the link is shared between the two directions. This sharing can occur in two ways: either the link must contain two physically separate transmission paths (one for sending and one for receiving), or the capacity of the single path is split between signals traveling in opposite directions.
  • Examples: Telephone network, modern switched Ethernet, mobile phone communication.

  Bidirectional  │   Device A   │                  │   Device B   │
                 │              │ ←─────────────── │              │
                 └──────────────┘                  └──────────────┘

Analog to Digital Conversion (ADC)

An Analog-to-Digital System converts continuous physical signals (like voice or temperature) into discrete binary representations. This process involves three essential steps:

  1. Sampling: Measuring the amplitude of the continuous analog signal at regular time intervals (sampling period $T_s$). The rate of sampling is $f_s = 1/T_s$.
  2. Quantization: Mapping the continuous amplitude values to the nearest discrete levels from a finite set of predetermined levels (L levels). This introduces a small error known as quantization noise.
  3. Coding: Assigning an n-bit binary code to represent each quantized amplitude level, where $L = 2^n$.

Advantages of Digital Transmission over Analog

  • Noise Immunity: Digital signals are less susceptible to noise and interference because they use discrete states (0 and 1). A slight modification of the waveform shape due to noise does not change the logic level unless the noise amplitude is very high.
  • Regeneration: Instead of amplifying an analog signal (which also amplifies the noise), digital systems use regenerative repeaters. These repeaters detect the binary values, discard the accumulated noise, and transmit clean, newly generated pulses.
  • Error Detection and Correction: Redundant bits can be added to digital data, allowing the receiver to identify and even correct transmission errors.
  • Compression and Cryptography: Digital data can be easily compressed to conserve bandwidth and encrypted for secure transmission.
  • Integration of Services: Voice, data, video, and control signals can be multiplexed and transmitted over the same digital network.
  • Cost-Effectiveness: Digital integrated circuits (ICs) are cheaper to manufacture and maintain than high-precision analog circuitry.

1.8 Nyquist and Shannon Theorems (Channel Capacity)

Nyquist Sampling Theorem

The Nyquist Sampling Theorem states that a continuous-time bandlimited signal containing no frequency components higher than $f_{max}$ Hz can be uniquely and completely reconstructed from its samples if it is sampled at a rate $f_s$ that is greater than or equal to twice the maximum frequency component: 

  fs ≥ 2 × fmax

  Where:
    fs   = Sampling frequency (samples per second)
    fmax = Highest frequency component of the analog signal
    2 × fmax is known as the "Nyquist Rate".

If the sampling rate is less than $2f_{max}$, high-frequency components overlap with low-frequency components in the reconstructed signal. This distortion is called aliasing.

Nyquist Bit Rate (Noiseless Channel)

For a noiseless (ideal) channel of bandwidth $B$, the maximum bit rate ($R$) at which data can be transmitted is limited by the number of signal levels ($L$) used: 

  Bit Rate (R) = 2 × B × log₂ (L) bps

  Where:
    B = Bandwidth of the channel in Hz
    L = Number of discrete signal levels (voltage levels)
    R = Maximum bit rate in bits per second (bps)
    log₂ (L) = Number of bits represented by each signal level (n)

Shannon Channel Capacity Theorem (Noisy Channel)

In reality, communication channels are noisy. Claude Shannon developed a formula to calculate the absolute maximum theoretical bit rate (Channel Capacity, $C$) of a noisy channel with bandwidth $B$ and Signal-to-Noise Ratio ($SNR$): 

  Capacity (C) = B × log₂ (1 + SNR) bps

  Where:
    B   = Bandwidth of the channel in Hz
    SNR = Signal-to-Noise Ratio (ratio of signal power to noise power, S/N)
    C   = Maximum channel capacity in bits per second (bps)

Important Note: Signal-to-Noise Ratio is often expressed in decibels (dB). Before applying Shannon's formula, you must convert the decibel value to a linear ratio:
$$SNR_{dB} = 10 \log_{10}(SNR) \implies SNR = 10^{\frac{SNR_{dB}}{10}}$$

Bit Rate vs. Baud Rate

  • Bit Rate: The number of data bits transmitted per second (bps). It measures the speed of data transfer.
  • Baud Rate: The number of signal changes (symbol changes) per second. It measures the modulation rate.
  • Relationship: Bit Rate = Baud Rate × $\log_2(L)$ or Bit Rate = Baud Rate × $n$, where $n$ is the number of bits per signal element.

1.9 Numerical Examples and Calculations

Numerical Example 1: Sampling Rate

Problem: A human voice signal has a frequency range from 300 Hz to 3400 Hz. What is the minimum sampling rate required to digitize this signal without aliasing?

Solution:
The highest frequency component is $f_{max} = 3400$ Hz.
According to Nyquist Sampling Theorem:
$f_s \ge 2 \times f_{max}$
$f_s \ge 2 \times 3400 = 6800$ Hz (samples/second).
Note: In standard telephone networks, the voice signal is sampled at 8000 Hz to provide a guard band.

Numerical Example 2: Nyquist Bit Rate

Problem: Consider a noiseless channel with a bandwidth of 3000 Hz transmitting a signal with 4 levels. Calculate the maximum bit rate.

Solution:
Bandwidth $B = 3000$ Hz.
Number of signal levels $L = 4$.
According to Nyquist Bit Rate formula:
$R = 2 \times B \times \log_2(L)$
$R = 2 \times 3000 \times \log_2(4) = 6000 \times 2 = 12000$ bps (12 kbps).

Numerical Example 3: Shannon Capacity

Problem: A telephone line has a bandwidth of 3100 Hz. The Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) is 30 dB. Calculate the maximum channel capacity.

Solution:
Bandwidth $B = 3100$ Hz.
Signal-to-Noise Ratio in dB, $SNR_{dB} = 30$ dB.
First, convert $SNR_{dB}$ to its linear ratio form:
$30 = 10 \log_{10}(SNR) \implies 3 = \log_{10}(SNR) \implies SNR = 10^3 = 1000$.
Now apply Shannon's formula:
$C = B \times \log_2(1 + SNR)$
$C = 3100 \times \log_2(1 + 1000) = 3100 \times \log_2(1001)$
Using base change rule: $\log_2(1001) = \frac{\log_{10}(1001)}{\log_{10}(2)} \approx \frac{3.00043}{0.30103} \approx 9.967$
$C = 3100 \times 9.967 \approx 30897.7$ bps $\approx 30.9$ kbps.

Numerical Example 4: Combined Limits

Problem: A channel has a bandwidth of 1 MHz. The SNR is 24 dB.
1. Calculate the maximum Shannon capacity.
2. To achieve this capacity, how many signal levels must be used according to Nyquist?

Solution:
Bandwidth $B = 1$ MHz = $10^6$ Hz.
$SNR_{dB} = 24$ dB.
Convert to linear ratio: $SNR = 10^{2.4} \approx 251.19$.

1. Shannon Capacity:
$C = B \times \log_2(1 + SNR) = 10^6 \times \log_2(1 + 251.19)$
$C = 10^6 \times \log_2(252.19) \approx 10^6 \times 7.978 \approx 7.98$ Mbps.

2. Nyquist Signal Levels:
We want to find $L$ such that Nyquist Bit Rate equals the Shannon capacity limit:
$7.98 \times 10^6 = 2 \times B \times \log_2(L)$
$7.98 \times 10^6 = 2 \times 10^6 \times \log_2(L)$
$7.98 = 2 \log_2(L) \implies \log_2(L) = 3.99 \approx 4$ bits per signal element.
$L = 2^{3.99} \approx 16$ levels.
Therefore, we must use 16 distinct signal levels to approach this capacity.

1.13 Line Coding Schemes

Line coding is the process of converting digital data (binary bit stream) into digital signals (voltage pulses). There are three major categories of line coding:

1. Unipolar Scheme

All signal levels are on one side of the time axis — either positive or zero.

  • Unipolar NRZ (Non-Return-to-Zero): High voltage represents 1, and zero voltage represents 0. The signal does not return to zero in the middle of a bit.
    Drawback: DC component is present, and synchronization loss occurs during long sequences of 0s or 1s.
  Bit Stream:    1        0        1        1        0
              ┌───────┐        ┌────────────────┐
  Voltage:    │       │        │                │
              ┘       └────────┘                └────────

2. Polar Schemes

Voltages are on both sides of the time axis (positive and negative levels).

NRZ-L (NRZ-Level) and NRZ-I (NRZ-Invert)

  • NRZ-L: The level of the voltage determines the value of the bit. Typically, positive voltage represents 0, and negative voltage represents 1.
  • NRZ-I: The inversion or lack of inversion determines the value of the bit. A transition at the beginning of the bit interval represents a 1; no transition represents a 0.
  Bit Stream:       1        0        1        1        0
  NRZ-L:       ----------            ------------------- (Negative = 1)
                         ------------                    (Positive = 0)

  NRZ-I:       |---------|-----------|_________|--------- (Transitions on 1)
  (starts high)           (no change) (change)  (change)

RZ (Return-to-Zero)

The signal changes not between bits, but during the bit. The signal returns to zero at the midpoint of each bit interval.

  • 1 is represented by positive-to-zero transition.
  • 0 is represented by negative-to-zero transition.
  • Advantage: Excellent synchronization.
  • Disadvantage: Requires double the bandwidth.

Biphase: Manchester and Differential Manchester

  • Manchester: A transition occurs in the middle of each bit interval. A 0 is represented by a high-to-low transition (positive-to-negative). A 1 is represented by a low-to-high transition (negative-to-positive). Used in legacy Coaxial Ethernet (10Base5/10Base2).
  • Differential Manchester: The transition in the middle of the bit interval is used only for synchronization. The presence or absence of a transition at the beginning of the bit interval determines the bit value:
    • Transition at beginning = 0
    • No transition at beginning = 1
    Used in Token Ring (IEEE 802.5).
  Bit Stream:          0          1          0          0
  Manchester:      ┌────┐     ┌──┐  ┌──┐     ┌────┐     ┌────┐
  (High-to-Low = 0)│    │     │  │  │  │     │    │     │    │
  (Low-to-High = 1)     └────┘  └──┘  └────┘     └────┘     └───
  Mid-bit Trans:     ↓          ↓          ↓          ↓

3. Bipolar Schemes

There are three voltage levels: positive, negative, and zero.

  • AMI (Alternate Mark Inversion): Neutral zero voltage represents 0. Binary 1s (marks) are represented by alternating positive and negative voltages.
  Bit Stream:    1        0        1        0        1
              ┌───────┐        │                 ┌───────┐
  Voltage:    │       │        │                 │       │
              ┘       └────────┴────────┘        ┘       └───────
                               (Negative)       (Positive)

Scrambling Techniques (B8ZS and HDB3)

Bipolar AMI suffers from synchronization loss during long sequences of zeros. To solve this, scrambling replaces long strings of zeros with predefined code patterns containing violations:

  • B8ZS (Bipolar with 8-Zero Substitution): Used in North America. If eight consecutive zeros occur, they are replaced with the pattern 000VB0VB, where V is a bipolar violation (same polarity as previous 1) and B is a bipolar element (opposite polarity).
  • HDB3 (High-Density Bipolar 3-Zero): Used in Europe and Asia. If four consecutive zeros occur, they are replaced with 000V or B00V depending on the parity of 1s since the last substitution.

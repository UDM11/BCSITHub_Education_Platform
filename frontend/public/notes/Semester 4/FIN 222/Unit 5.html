Unit 5: Capital Structure and Financing Decision [10 Hrs.]

5.1 Capital Structure vs. Financial Structure

Definitions

  • Financial Structure: Represents the entire right-hand side of the Balance Sheet. It includes all sources of financing (both short-term current liabilities like accounts payable and long-term liabilities/equity).
  • Capital Structure: The permanent, long-term financing mix of the firm. It excludes short-term current liabilities.
    $$\text{Capital Structure} = \text{Long-term Debt} + \text{Preferred Stock} + \text{Common Equity}$$

Capital Structure Components

Component Cost Level Risk Level for Firm Key Characteristics
Debt Capital Lowest cost (interest is tax-deductible) Highest risk (fixed payment obligations; default leads to bankruptcy) No voting rights, no dilution of control, fixed maturity.
Preferred Stock Medium cost Medium risk (omitting dividend doesn't trigger default, but accumulates) Hybrid security, fixed dividend rate, no voting rights, priority over common stock.
Common Stock / Equity Highest cost (requires highest return) Lowest risk (no obligation to pay dividends or return capital) Represents true ownership, voting rights, receives residual profits, causes dilution.
Retained Earnings Slightly lower than new common stock (no flotation costs) Lowest risk Internal source of equity capital. Has opportunity cost ($r_s$).

5.2 Factors Affecting Capital Structure and Leverage Risks

Factors Influencing Capital Structure Decisions

  1. Taxes: Since interest payments are tax-deductible, higher corporate tax rates encourage firms to use more debt (interest tax shield benefit).
  2. Bankruptcy Costs: Highly volatile firms face a higher threat of bankruptcy, restricting them from borrowing heavily.
  3. Financial Flexibility: Firms should keep reserve borrowing capacity to exploit future opportunities.
  4. Management Attitudes: Aggressive managers borrow heavily; conservative ones avoid debt.

Business Risk vs. Financial Risk

  • Business Risk (Operating Leverage): The natural risk inherent in the firm's operations with zero debt financing. Driven by revenue volatility, input price changes, and the ratio of fixed operating costs to variable operating costs (high fixed costs lead to high operating leverage).
  • Financial Risk (Financial Leverage): The additional risk placed on common shareholders as a result of choosing to finance with debt (fixed interest obligations). High financial leverage increases the volatility of EPS and the risk of insolvency.

5.3 Component Cost of Capital Formulas

Before calculating the total WACC, we must calculate the individual cost of each component:

1. Cost of Debt ($r_d$)

The cost of borrowing long-term funds. Since interest is tax-deductible, we must find the **after-tax cost of debt**:

$$\text{After-tax } r_d = r_d \times (1 - T)$$ Where $r_d$ is the nominal interest rate on debt and $T$ is the corporate tax rate.

2. Cost of Preferred Stock ($r_{ps}$)

Preferred dividends are paid out of after-tax earnings, so there is no tax shield:

$$r_{ps} = \frac{D_{ps}}{P_p - F} = \frac{D_{ps}}{NP}$$ Where $D_{ps}$ is the annual preferred dividend, $P_p$ is the market price, $F$ is flotation cost, and $NP$ is Net Proceeds.

3. Cost of Retained Earnings / Common Equity ($r_s$)

Can be calculated using three main methods:

  • CAPM Method:
    $$r_s = r_{RF} + (r_M - r_{RF}) \times \beta$$
  • Dividend Growth Model (Gordon Growth Model):
    $$r_s = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g$$ Where $D_1$ is expected dividend next year ($D_0 \times (1+g)$), $P_0$ is current stock price, and $g$ is constant growth rate.
  • Bond Yield Plus Risk Premium Method:
    $$r_s = \text{Bond Yield} + \text{Risk Premium} \approx r_d + (3\% \text{ to } 5\%)$$

4. Cost of New Common Stock ($r_e$)

Includes flotation costs ($F$) incurred when issuing new shares to the public:

$$r_e = \frac{D_1}{P_0 \times (1 - F_{percent})} + g = \frac{D_1}{NP} + g$$

5.4 Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)

The **WACC** represents the overall average rate a business pays to finance its assets. It is calculated by multiplying the individual cost of each capital component by its proportional weight:

$$WACC = w_d \cdot r_d (1 - T) + w_{ps} \cdot r_{ps} + w_s \cdot r_s$$ Where $w_d, w_{ps}, w_s$ represent the weights of debt, preferred stock, and common equity respectively.

WACC Calculation Worked Numerical

Problem: Kathmandu Enterprise has the following capital structure and cost parameters:
- Debt: Book value = $3,000,000. Stated interest rate is 10%.
- Preferred Stock: Book value = $1,000,000. Pays an 8% dividend on $100 par value. Market price is $95.
- Common Equity: Book value = $6,000,000. Market price $P_0 = \$40$. Most recent dividend paid $D_0 = \$2.00$ per share, and dividends are expected to grow at a constant rate of 6% per year.
- Beta of the stock is 1.2, risk-free rate is 5%, market return is 11%.
- Corporate Tax Rate $T = 30\%$.
Calculate WACC using CAPM for the cost of equity.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate Weights:
Total Capital = Debt + Preferred + Common = $3,000,000 + 1,000,000 + 6,000,000 = \$10,000,000$.
- Weight of Debt $w_d = 3M / 10M = 0.30$.
- Weight of Preferred $w_{ps} = 1M / 10M = 0.10$.
- Weight of Common Equity $w_s = 6M / 10M = 0.60$.

Step 2: Calculate Component Costs:
1. After-tax Cost of Debt:
$r_d(after-tax) = 10\% \times (1 - 0.30) = 7.0\%$.

2. Cost of Preferred Stock:
$D_{ps} = \$100 \text{ par} \times 8\% = \$8$.
$r_{ps} = D_{ps} / P_p = \$8 / \$95 \approx 0.0842 \quad (8.42\%)$.

3. Cost of Equity ($r_s$) using CAPM:
$r_s = r_{RF} + (r_M - r_{RF}) \times \beta = 5\% + (11\% - 5\%) \times 1.2 = 5\% + 6\% \times 1.2 = 5\% + 7.2\% = 12.2\%$.
(Check using Dividend Growth Model for comparison: $D_1 = D_0(1+g) = 2(1.06) = \$2.12$. $r_s = (2.12 / 40) + 0.06 = 0.053 + 0.06 = 11.3\%$. We will proceed with CAPM 12.2% as requested).

Step 3: Calculate WACC:
$WACC = (w_d \times \text{after-tax } r_d) + (w_{ps} \times r_{ps}) + (w_s \times r_s)$
$WACC = (0.30 \times 7.0\%) + (0.10 \times 8.42\%) + (0.60 \times 12.2\%)$
$WACC = 2.10\% + 0.842\% + 7.32\% = 10.262\%$.
The Weighted Average Cost of Capital is 10.26%.

5.5 Capital Structure Theories

Several theories explain the relationship between capital structure, cost of capital, and firm value:

  1. Net Income (NI) Approach: Suggests that value can be increased and cost of capital decreased by increasing the proportion of cheaper debt in the capital structure. (Optimal capital structure exists at 100% debt).
  2. Net Operating Income (NOI) Approach: Argues that WACC remains constant regardless of leverage because equity holders demand higher returns as risk increases, completely offsetting debt benefits. (Optimal capital structure does not exist).
  3. Modigliani-Miller (MM) Hypotheses:
    • MM Proposition I (Without Taxes): In perfect markets, the value of a firm is independent of its leverage.
    • MM Proposition II (With Taxes): Because interest is tax-deductible, corporate taxes create a **tax shield** that increases firm value as debt increases:
      $$V_L = V_U + T \cdot D$$ Where $V_L$ is leverage value, $V_U$ is unleveraged value, $T$ is tax rate, and $D$ is debt value.
  4. Trade-off Theory: Suggests that firms trade off the tax benefits of debt against the costs of financial distress and bankruptcy. The optimal structure occurs where the marginal tax benefit equals marginal bankruptcy costs.
  5. Pecking Order Theory: Suggests that firms prefer internal financing (retained earnings) first, debt financing second, and new equity issue (common stock) as a last resort due to asymmetric information and flotation costs.

5.6 Leverage Analysis (DOL, DFL, DTL)

Leverage measures the sensitivity of a firm's bottom-line profits to changes in top-line sales:

  • Degree of Operating Leverage (DOL): Measures the sensitivity of EBIT to changes in Sales.
    $$DOL = \frac{\% \Delta EBIT}{\% \Delta Sales} = \frac{Q(P - V)}{Q(P - V) - FC} = \frac{\text{Contribution Margin}}{EBIT}$$
  • Degree of Financial Leverage (DFL): Measures the sensitivity of Net Income (or EPS) to changes in EBIT.
    $$DFL = \frac{\% \Delta EPS}{\% \Delta EBIT} = \frac{EBIT}{EBIT - I}$$
  • Degree of Total Leverage (DTL): Measures the sensitivity of EPS to changes in Sales.
    $$DTL = DOL \times DFL = \frac{\text{Contribution Margin}}{EBIT - I}$$

Leverage Calculations Worked Numerical

Problem: A firm has Net Sales of $500,000, Variable Cost of $300,000, Fixed Operating Cost of $120,000, and Interest Expense of $20,000.
1. Calculate DOL, DFL, and DTL.
2. If Sales increase by 10%, calculate the percentage increase in EBIT and EPS.

Solution:

1. Compute EBIT:
Contribution Margin = Sales - Variable Cost = $500,000 - 300,000 = $200,000.
EBIT = Contribution Margin - Fixed Cost = $200,000 - 120,000 = $80,000.
EBT = EBIT - Interest = $80,000 - 20,000 = $60,000.

2. Calculate Leverage:
- $DOL = \text{Contribution Margin} / EBIT = 200,000 / 80,000 = 2.5$.
- $DFL = EBIT / EBT = 80,000 / 60,000 \approx 1.33$.
- $DTL = DOL \times DFL = 2.5 \times 1.33 = 3.33$.

3. Impact of 10% Sales Increase:
- Increase in EBIT = $\% \Delta Sales \times DOL = 10\% \times 2.5 = 25\%$.
- Increase in EPS = $\% \Delta Sales \times DTL = 10\% \times 3.33 = 33.33\%$.

5.7 Operating and Financial Break-Even Analysis

Break-even analysis helps managers determine the sales volume or EBIT level required to avoid losses:

1. Operating Break-Even Point

The sales quantity where operating income (EBIT) is exactly zero. Fixed operating costs are fully covered:
$$Q_{BE} = \frac{FC}{P - V}$$ $$\text{Sales Value } S_{BE} = Q_{BE} \times P = \frac{FC}{1 - \frac{V}{P}} = \frac{FC}{\text{Contribution Margin Ratio}}$$ Where $FC$ is fixed operating cost, $P$ is unit price, and $V$ is unit variable cost.

2. Financial Break-Even Point

The EBIT level where Net Income (and EPS) is exactly zero. It covers fixed financial costs (interest and preferred dividends):
$$\text{Financial Break-Even EBIT} = I + \frac{D_{ps}}{1 - T}$$ Where $I$ is interest expense, $D_{ps}$ is preferred dividends, and $T$ is tax rate.

Break-Even Worked Numerical

Problem: A firm has fixed operating costs of $60,000. It sells products at $10 each, with unit variable costs of $6. The firm has an interest expense of $10,000, preferred dividends of $3,500, and a tax rate of 30%. Calculate: (1) Operating break-even quantity, and (2) Financial break-even EBIT.

Solution:
1. Operating Break-Even:
$$Q_{BE} = \frac{FC}{P - V} = \frac{60,000}{10 - 6} = \frac{60,000}{4} = 15,000 \text{ units}$$

2. Financial Break-Even:
$$\text{EBIT} = I + \frac{D_{ps}}{1 - T} = 10,000 + \frac{3,500}{1 - 0.30} = 10,000 + \frac{3,500}{0.70} = 10,000 + 5,000 = \$15,000$$
The firm must earn an operating profit (EBIT) of at least $15,000 to cover interest and preferred dividends.

5.8 EBIT-EPS Indifference Analysis

The **EBIT-EPS Indifference Point** is the level of EBIT at which the Earnings Per Share (EPS) remains identical under two different financing plans. It assists managers in selecting the optimal capital plan:

Indifference Formula

$$\frac{(EBIT - I_1)(1 - T) - D_{ps1}}{N_1} = \frac{(EBIT - I_2)(1 - T) - D_{ps2}}{N_2}$$ Where:
- $I_1, I_2$ are interest expenses under Plan 1 and Plan 2.
- $N_1, N_2$ are the number of outstanding common shares under Plan 1 and Plan 2.
- $D_{ps1}, D_{ps2}$ are preferred dividends under both plans.

EBIT-EPS Indifference Worked Example

Problem: Nepal Tech is considering two plans to raise $100,000:
- **Plan 1 (All Equity):** Issue 10,000 common shares. No debt.
- **Plan 2 (Debt-Equity):** Issue 5,000 common shares and borrow $50,000 at 10% interest ($I_2 = \$5,000$).
Corporate tax rate $T = 30\%$. Find the EBIT indifference level.

Solution:
Plan 1: $I_1 = 0$, $N_1 = 10,000$.
Plan 2: $I_2 = 5,000$, $N_2 = 5,000$.
Set up equation:
$$\frac{(EBIT - 0)(1 - 0.30)}{10,000} = \frac{(EBIT - 5,000)(1 - 0.30)}{5,000}$$
Divide both sides by 0.70 and multiply by 10,000:
$$EBIT = 2 \times (EBIT - 5,000)$$
$$EBIT = 2 \cdot EBIT - 10,000 \implies EBIT = \$10,000$$
The indifference EBIT level is $10,000. If EBIT is expected to exceed $10,000, Plan 2 (Debt) is preferred because it yields higher EPS; if below, Plan 1 (All Equity) is preferred.

5.9 Capital Cost Adjustments in the Service and IT Sectors

Determining the WACC for software development and IT service companies requires specific operational adjustments:

  • Absence of Cheap Debt: Because tech startups lack physical collateral (like land/factories) to back loans, commercial banks charge high risk premiums or reject loan requests. As a result, the debt weight ($w_d$) is usually very low (0% to 15%).
  • High Equity Cost ($r_s$): Tech stocks are volatile, resulting in high Betas ($\beta \ge 1.3$). Using CAPM, this high Beta increases the cost of equity. Since WACC is heavily weighted towards expensive equity, IT firm WACCs are typically high (12% to 16%) compared to utilities (6% to 8%).
  • Implications: A high WACC raises the hurdle rate for capital budgeting. Software projects must deliver very high rates of return to show positive NPVs.

5.10 Marginal Cost of Capital (MCC) and Capital Break Points

The **Marginal Cost of Capital (MCC)** is the cost of the last dollar of new capital raised by the firm. As a firm raises larger sums, the cost of individual components increases (e.g., bank loans interest rates rise, or the firm exhausts retained earnings and must issue new common stock with high flotation costs).

  • Capital Break Point: The total amount of new capital at which WACC increases:
    $$\text{Break Point} = \frac{\text{Limit of cheaper component source}}{\text{Weight of component in WACC}}$$

MCC Break Point Worked Example

Problem: A firm has a capital structure of 40% Debt and 60% Common Equity. The firm expects to generate $120,000 of retained earnings this year. Beyond $120,000, it must issue new common stock with a flotation cost of 10%, which increases the cost of equity. Calculate the capital break point at which the WACC will increase.

Solution:
The cheaper equity source is Retained Earnings (limit = $120,000).
The weight of equity in the WACC is $w_s = 60\% = 0.60$.

$$\text{Break Point}_{\text{Equity}} = \frac{\text{Retained Earnings}}{\text{Weight of Equity}} = \frac{120,000}{0.60} = \$200,000$$
If the firm raises up to $200,000 of total capital, it will use $80,000 debt (40%) and $120,000 retained earnings (60%). Beyond $200,000, the retained earnings are exhausted, and it must issue new equity, causing the WACC to rise.

5.11 The Optimum Capital Budget

Firms determine their optimal investment volume by plotting the **MCC Schedule** (which slopes upward as WACC increases at break points) against the **Investment Opportunity Schedule (IOS)** (which slopes downward as projects are ranked and selected from highest IRR to lowest IRR): 

  Percent (%)
    ▲
    │          / MCC (Cost of Capital rises)
    │   \     /
    │    \   /  <-- Intersection (Optimum Capital Budget)
    │     \ /
    │      X
    │     / \
    │    /   \  IOS (Project IRRs ranked)
    └────┴────┴──────────────────────────► Capital Raised ($)

Decision Rule: Accept all projects whose IRR exceeds the marginal cost of capital. The intersection of the MCC and IOS schedules defines the firm's **Optimum Capital Budget**.

5.12 Trade-Off Theory Curve and Signaling Effects

1. Trade-Off Theory Curve Analysis

The Trade-off Theory states that the value of a leveraged firm is equal to the value of an unleveraged firm plus the present value of the interest tax shield minus the present value of financial distress costs: 

  Firm Value ($)
    ▲                / [Value with tax shield: VL = VU + TD]
    │               /
    │         __--X--__  <-- Optimal Point (Marginal Tax Shield = Marginal Distress Cost)
    │     __--    │    --__
    │   _--       │        --__ [Actual Value (leveraged)]
    │  /          │            --
 VU ┼─o───────────┼───────────────► Debt/Equity Ratio (%)
    │             Optimal Debt
  • Initial Phase: As debt increases, tax shield benefits dominate, raising firm value.
  • Optimal Point: The maximum point of the curve where the marginal tax shield benefit matches the marginal increase in bankruptcy costs.
  • Decline Phase: Beyond this point, the probability and cost of financial distress (bankruptcy threats, lost sales, supplier restrictions) outweigh tax benefits, depressing firm value.

2. Signaling Theory in Financing Decisions

Because managers have better information than outside investors (**Asymmetric Information**), their financing decisions send signals to the market:

  • Debt Issuance (Positive Signal): Indicates that managers believe the firm has stable future cash flows and can easily cover fixed interest payments. Lenders interpret this as a sign of financial strength.
  • Equity Issuance (Negative/Bearish Signal): Indicates that managers believe the stock is currently **overvalued** and are trying to sell expensive shares to the public. As a result, stock prices typically drop upon announcement of a new share issue.

5.13 Agency Costs of Debt and Equity

Capital structure decisions also affect agency conflicts within the firm:

  • Agency Costs of Equity: When managers do not own the entire company, they may not work as hard or may waste corporate resources on personal perks (luxurious offices, company cars). Debt reduces this risk because the legal obligation to make interest payments forces managers to run a lean operation to avoid bankruptcy. This is called the **disciplining role of debt**.
  • Agency Costs of Debt: When a firm borrows money, shareholders have an incentive to invest in highly risky projects because they capture all the upside while creditors absorb the losses if the project fails. Lenders prevent this by charging higher interest rates or imposing restrictive covenants, which limits the firm's operating flexibility.

3. The Free Cash Flow Hypothesis

Michael Jensen's **Free Cash Flow Hypothesis** states that managers with high levels of free cash flow (cash beyond what is needed to fund all positive-NPV projects) have a tendency to waste it on low-yielding or value-destroying projects (e.g., empire-building acquisitions). By issuing debt instead of equity, the firm obligates itself to pay out this cash as interest, reducing the amount of free cash flow available to managers to waste.

4. Types of Debt Covenants

Lenders write protective covenants into credit agreements to limit agency costs of debt:

  • Negative Covenants (Restrictions): Limit the firm's actions (e.g., prohibiting additional debt, capping dividend payments, restricting asset sales, or preventing mergers without lender approval).
  • Positive Covenants (Requirements): Obligate the firm to take specific actions (e.g., maintaining a minimum current ratio of 1.5, submitting audited financial statements quarterly, or keeping key asset insurance active).

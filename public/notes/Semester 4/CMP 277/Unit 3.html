Unit 3: Physical Layer and its Design Issues [7 Hrs.]

3.1 Duties of Physical Layer and Transmission Media

Role and Design Issues

The Physical Layer is the lowest layer of the OSI architecture. Its primary purpose is to transmit individual bits over a physical medium from one node to the next. Its design issues center around the mechanical, electrical, functional, and procedural interfaces.

  • Data Rate: Defining the transmission speed (number of bits per second).
  • Bit Representation: Defining how 0s and 1s are encoded (voltage levels).
  • Bit Synchronization: Aligning transmitter and receiver clocks.
  • Line Configuration: Connection type (point-to-point or multipoint).
  • Physical Topology: How devices are physically wired.
  • Transmission Mode: Simplex, half-duplex, or full-duplex.

Guided Transmission Media (Wired)

Guided media provide a physical pathway for electromagnetic waves to travel.

1. Twisted-Pair Cable

Consists of two copper conductors, each with its own plastic insulation, twisted together. Twisting reduces crosstalk (electromagnetic interference from adjacent pairs).

  • UTP (Unshielded Twisted Pair): Cheap, flexible, but susceptible to external noise. Commonly classified by categories (Cat 5e, Cat 6, Cat 6a).
  • STP (Shielded Twisted Pair): Contains a metal braid jacket to protect against noise, but is bulky and expensive.

2. Coaxial Cable

Contains a central copper core surrounded by an insulating layer, covered by a braided metal shield and an outer jacket. Historically used in cable TV and early Ethernet.

3. Optical Fiber Cable

Transmits data as light pulses through glass or plastic cores. It works on the principle of Total Internal Reflection.

  • Single-Mode Fiber (SMF): Thin core (9µm), single light ray path, uses laser sources, suitable for long distances (low attenuation).
  • Multi-Mode Fiber (MMF): Thicker core (50µm or 62.5µm), multiple light paths, uses LED sources, cheaper but prone to modal dispersion (limited distance).

Unguided Transmission Media (Wireless)

Unguided media transmit electromagnetic waves without a physical conductor.

  • Radio Waves: Electromagnetic waves between 3 kHz and 1 GHz. Omnidirectional propagation, easily pass through walls, used for FM radio, Wi-Fi.
  • Microwaves: Between 1 GHz and 300 GHz. Unidirectional (line-of-sight), highly focused, used for terrestrial microwave towers and satellite links.
  • Infrared: Between 300 GHz and 400 THz. Short-range, line-of-sight, cannot penetrate walls, used for remote controls.
  • Satellite Communication: Satellites receive signals from ground stations, amplify them, and rebroadcast them over a wide area (transponders). Common orbits: GEO (35,786 km), MEO, LEO.

  • Straight-Through Cable: Both ends use T568B (or both T568A). Used to connect different devices (e.g., PC to Switch).
  • Crossover Cable: One end uses T568A, the other uses T568B. Used to connect similar devices (e.g., PC to PC, Switch to Switch).

Ethernet Speeds

  • Standard Ethernet (10Base-T): 10 Mbps speed, baseband transmission, twisted-pair copper.
  • Fast Ethernet (100Base-TX): 100 Mbps speed, uses 2-pair Cat 5 or better cables.
  • Gigabit Ethernet (1000Base-T): 1 Gbps speed, uses all 4 pairs of Cat 5e/Cat 6.

Wireless Standards

  • IEEE 802.11 (Wi-Fi): Defines WLAN standards.
    • 802.11a: 54 Mbps, 5 GHz
    • 802.11b: 11 Mbps, 2.4 GHz
    • 802.11g: 54 Mbps, 2.4 GHz
    • 802.11n: 600 Mbps, 2.4/5 GHz (uses MIMO)
    • 802.11ac: 1.3 Gbps, 5 GHz
    • 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6): Up to 9.6 Gbps, high density
  • IEEE 802.15.1 (Bluetooth): Low-power, short-range PAN wireless standard. Operates in 2.4 GHz ISM band using FHSS (Frequency Hopping Spread Spectrum).

3.3 Signals and Transmission Impairments

Signals

  • Analog Signal: Continuous values that vary smoothly over time.
  • Digital Signal: Discrete levels representing binary values.
  • Periodic Signal: Repeats a pattern over a regular interval called a period ($T$). Frequency is $f = 1/T$.
  • Non-periodic Signal: Does not repeat a pattern over time.

Transmission Impairments

Signals travel through transmission media, which are not perfect. The imperfection causes signal degradation, leading to three types of impairments:

1. Attenuation

Loss of signal energy over distance due to resistance of the medium. Measured in decibels (dB): 

  Decibel (dB) = 10 × log₁₀ (P_out / P_in)

  Where:
    P_in  = Input power
    P_out = Output power

  If the signal power is halved: 10 × log₁₀ (0.5) ≈ -3 dB. A -3 dB change represents loss of 50% power.

2. Distortion

Occurs when the signal changes its shape or form. Each frequency component of a composite analog signal travels at a slightly different speed, arriving at the destination with different phase shifts.

3. Noise

Unwanted signals that combine with the original signal:

  • Thermal Noise: Random motion of electrons in conductors (proportional to temperature).
  • Induced Noise: Comes from external sources like motors or fluorescent lights.
  • Crosstalk: Interference caused by electromagnetic coupling between adjacent cables.
  • Impulse Noise: Short spikes of high energy caused by lighting, power line surges, etc.

3.4 Multiplexing and Switching Techniques

Multiplexing

The set of techniques that allows the simultaneous transmission of multiple signals across a single data link.

1. FDM (Frequency Division Multiplexing)

Analog technique. The available bandwidth of a single link is divided into multiple overlapping frequency bands, each assigned to a separate channel. Guard bands are used to prevent interference. Used in radio and cable TV.

2. TDM (Time Division Multiplexing)

Digital technique. The entire bandwidth of the link is shared in time. Users are assigned dedicated time slots. Can be Synchronous (fixed slots) or Statistical (dynamic slots).

3. WDM (Wavelength Division Multiplexing)

Analog technique designed for optical fibers. Multiple light beams of different wavelengths (colors) are combined into a single fiber and demultiplexed at the receiving end.

Switching

Switching determines how data is routed through intermediate nodes in a network:

3.5 Network Performance Metrics

Performance metrics quantify how well a network transfers data:

  • Bandwidth: The maximum amount of data that can pass through a channel per unit time (Hz or bps).
  • Throughput: The actual amount of data successfully transmitted over the network in a given period. Throughput is usually lower than bandwidth due to overhead, collisions, and impairments.
  • Latency (Delay): The total time required for a packet to travel from source to destination.
    $$\text{Latency} = \text{Propagation Delay} + \text{Transmission Delay} + \text{Queuing Delay} + \text{Processing Delay}$$
    • Propagation Delay: Time for a bit to travel the physical distance.
      $$\text{Prop. Delay} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Speed of Signal in Medium}}$$ Note: Speed of light in copper/fiber $\approx 2 \times 10^8$ m/s.
    • Transmission Delay: Time required to push all bits of a packet onto the medium.
      $$\text{Trans. Delay} = \frac{\text{Packet Size (bits)}}{\text{Transmission Rate (bps)}}$$
    • Queuing Delay: Time a packet spends in buffers waiting to be processed.
    • Processing Delay: Time routers take to examine packet headers and determine the destination.
  • Bandwidth-Delay Product (BDP): The volume of bits that can fill the transmission link at any instant:
    $$\text{BDP} = \text{Bandwidth} \times \text{Propagation Delay}$$
  • Jitter: The variation or fluctuation in packet arrival times. High jitter degrades real-time applications like VoIP.

Performance Numerical Example

Problem: A file of size 2 Megabytes (2 MB) is sent over a link with a distance of 10,000 km, bandwidth of 10 Mbps, and signal propagation speed of $2 \times 10^8$ m/s. Calculate the total latency (excluding queuing and processing delays).

Solution:
File size = 2 MB = $2 \times 2^{20} \times 8$ bits = $16,777,216$ bits.
Bandwidth = 10 Mbps = $10^7$ bps.
Distance = 10,000 km = $10^7$ meters.
Speed = $2 \times 10^8$ m/s.

1. Propagation Delay:
$\text{Prop. Delay} = \text{Distance} / \text{Speed} = 10^7 / (2 \times 10^8) = 0.05$ seconds = 50 ms.

2. Transmission Delay:
$\text{Trans. Delay} = \text{File Size} / \text{Bandwidth} = 16,777,216 / 10^7 \approx 1.677$ seconds.

Total Latency:
$\text{Latency} = 0.05 + 1.677 = 1.727$ seconds.

3.6 Wireless Propagation Modes

Electromagnetic waves travel from source to destination in space through different modes depending on their frequency:

  • Ground Wave Propagation: Follows the curvature of the Earth. Used for low-frequency signals (frequencies below 2 MHz) like AM radio. The waves hug the surface, wrapping around obstacles, but lose signal strength rapidly due to absorption by the ground.
  • Sky Wave Propagation: Utilizes the ionosphere. High-frequency signals (2 MHz to 30 MHz) are radiated upward and reflected back to Earth by the charged ionosphere. Allows long-distance, intercontinental communication (e.g., shortwave radio).
  • Space Wave Propagation: Line-of-sight propagation. Very high-frequency signals (above 30 MHz) cannot be reflected by the ionosphere and must travel in straight paths directly from transmitter to receiver. Earth's curvature limits the distance, requiring high repeaters/towers or satellites.

3.8 Practical Decibel Calculations (Power Losses and Gains)

In communication systems, signals experience cascading losses (cables) and gains (amplifiers). Decibels allow simple addition and subtraction instead of multiplication and division.

Numerical Example: Multi-Stage Link Analysis

Problem: A signal with an initial power of 10 mW is sent over a 10 km fiber optic cable having an attenuation of 0.5 dB/km. The signal is then passed through an amplifier with a gain of 15 dB. Calculate the final output power.

Solution:
1. Initial power $P_{in} = 10$ mW.
Convert to dBm (decibels relative to 1 milliwatt):
$P_{in}(dBm) = 10 \log_{10}(10\text{ mW} / 1\text{ mW}) = 10 \log_{10}(10) = 10$ dBm.

2. Total fiber cable loss:
$\text{Loss} = 10\text{ km} \times 0.5\text{ dB/km} = 5$ dB (negative gain).

3. Amplifier gain:
$\text{Gain} = 15$ dB.

4. Calculate final power in dBm:
$P_{out}(dBm) = P_{in}(dBm) - \text{Loss} + \text{Gain}$
$P_{out}(dBm) = 10\text{ dBm} - 5\text{ dB} + 15\text{ dB} = 20$ dBm.

5. Convert back to linear power:
$20\text{ dBm} = 10 \log_{10}(P_{out}\text{ in mW}) \implies 2 = \log_{10}(P_{out}) \implies P_{out} = 10^2 = 100$ mW.
The final output power of the signal is 100 mW.

3.9 Physical Interface Standards

The physical layer also defines mechanical and electrical standards for connecting devices to the transmission medium:

  • RS-232 Standard: A classic serial interface standard defined by EIA for connecting DTE (Data Terminal Equipment, e.g., computer) and DCE (Data Circuit-terminating Equipment, e.g., modem). Uses a 25-pin DB-25 connector or a 9-pin DB-9 connector. It defines signal functions and electrical levels (e.g., control lines, Tx, Rx, ground).
  • DB-9 Pin Configuration:
    • Pin 1: DCD (Data Carrier Detect)
    • Pin 2: RxD (Receive Data)
    • Pin 3: TxD (Transmit Data)
    • Pin 4: DTR (Data Terminal Ready)
    • Pin 5: GND (Signal Ground)
    • Pin 6: DSR (Data Set Ready)
    • Pin 7: RTS (Request to Send)
    • Pin 8: CTS (Clear to Send)
    • Pin 9: RI (Ring Indicator)
  • USB (Universal Serial Bus): A modern hot-pluggable serial interface standard. It supports four transfer rates: Low-speed (1.5 Mbps), Full-speed (12 Mbps), High-speed (480 Mbps), and Super-speed (up to 20 Gbps for USB 3.2). Uses differential signaling on a 4-wire bus (Vbus, D+, D-, GND).
  • RJ-45 (Registered Jack 45): An 8-pin/8-position physical connector used in twisted-pair Ethernet cables.

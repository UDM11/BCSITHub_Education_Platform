3.1 Concept of Time Value of Money and Timelines

What is the Time Value of Money (TVM)?

The **Time Value of Money (TVM)** is the core principle of finance stating that a dollar in hand today is worth more than a dollar to be received in the future. This is true due to three primary reasons:

  • Opportunity Cost (Interest / Investment): Money in hand today can be invested to earn interest, growing into a larger amount.
  • Inflation: Inflation erodes the purchasing power of money over time, meaning a dollar in the future buys fewer goods than a dollar today.
  • Risk / Uncertainty: Future cash flows are uncertain and risky. Cash today has zero default risk.

Cash Flow Timelines

A **cash flow timeline** is an essential tool used to visualize the timing of cash inflows and outflows. Tick marks represent the end of periods: 

  Example Timeline: 5-Year project with a $1,000 initial investment and $300 annual inflows.

  Period:     0          1          2          3          4          5
              ├──────────┼──────────┼──────────┼──────────┼──────────┤
  Cash Flow: -$1,000    +$300      +$300      +$300      +$300      +$300

- Period 0 represents **today**. It is the start of Period 1.
- Period 1 tick mark represents the **end of Period 1** (or start of Period 2).

Types of Cash Flows

  • Single Cash Flow: A lump sum transaction occurring once (e.g., investing $1,000 today and receiving $1,500 in Year 5).
  • Annuity: A series of equal cash payments/receipts occurring at equal intervals over a finite period (e.g., $100 paid every year for 5 years).
    • Ordinary Annuity: Cash flows occur at the **end** of each period. (Standard bank loans, interest payments).
    • Annuity Due: Cash flows occur at the **beginning** of each period. (Rent payments, lease contracts, insurance premiums).
  • Uneven Cash Flows: A stream of cash flows where the payments vary in size (e.g., Year 1: $100, Year 2: $250, Year 3: $50).
  • Perpetuity: An annuity that continues indefinitely (forever) (e.g., preferred stock dividends).

3.2 Mathematical Formulas for PV and FV

Here are the mathematical formulas for valuing single amounts and annuities:

1. Single Cash Flows

  • Future Value (Compounding): Finding the value of a current sum in the future.
    $$FV_n = PV \times (1 + r)^n$$
  • Present Value (Discounting): Finding the value of a future sum today.
    $$PV = \frac{FV_n}{(1 + r)^n} = FV_n \times (1 + r)^{-n}$$

2. Annuities (Equal Cash Flows)

  • Future Value of an Ordinary Annuity (FVOA):
    $$FVOA_n = PMT \times \left[ \frac{(1 + r)^n - 1}{r} \right]$$
  • Future Value of an Annuity Due (FVAD): Since payments occur at the beginning, they earn interest for one extra period.
    $$FVAD_n = FVOA_n \times (1 + r) = PMT \times \left[ \frac{(1 + r)^n - 1}{r} \right] \times (1 + r)$$
  • Present Value of an Ordinary Annuity (PVOA):
    $$PVOA_n = PMT \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1 + r)^{-n}}{r} \right]$$
  • Present Value of an Annuity Due (PVAD):
    $$PVAD_n = PVOA_n \times (1 + r) = PMT \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1 + r)^{-n}}{r} \right] \times (1 + r)$$

3. Perpetuities (Infinite Streams)

The present value of a perpetuity is calculated as:
$$PV_{perpetuity} = \frac{PMT}{r}$$

3.3 Nominal, Periodic, and Effective Interest Rates

When interest is compounded more than once a year (e.g., semi-annually, quarterly, monthly), we must distinguish between different rates:

  • Nominal Interest Rate ($I_{NOM}$ / APR): The stated or advertised annual rate. It does not account for compounding within the year.
  • Periodic Interest Rate ($I_{PER}$): The rate charged per compounding period.
    $$I_{PER} = \frac{I_{NOM}}{m}$$ Where $m$ is the number of compounding periods per year (e.g., $m=2$ for semi-annual, $m=4$ for quarterly, $m=12$ for monthly).
  • Effective Annual Rate ($EAR$): The actual annual rate of interest earned, accounting for compounding. Used to compare loans with different compounding frequencies.
    $$EAR = \left( 1 + \frac{I_{NOM}}{m} \right)^m - 1$$

Worked Example: Rate Comparison

Problem: A bank offers a credit card with a nominal rate of 12% APR. Compare the Effective Annual Rate (EAR) under: (1) Semi-annual, (2) Quarterly, and (3) Monthly compounding.

Solution:
1. Semi-annual ($m=2$):
$EAR = (1 + 0.12/2)^2 - 1 = (1.06)^2 - 1 = 1.1236 - 1 = 0.1236 \quad (12.36\%)$.
2. Quarterly ($m=4$):
$EAR = (1 + 0.12/4)^4 - 1 = (1.03)^4 - 1 = 1.1255 - 1 = 0.1255 \quad (12.55\%)$.
3. Monthly ($m=12$):
$EAR = (1 + 0.12/12)^{12} - 1 = (1.01)^{12} - 1 = 1.1268 - 1 = 0.1268 \quad (12.68\%)$.
Conclusion: As compounding frequency increases, the effective rate of interest (EAR) increases.

3.4 Loan Amortization Schedule

An **amortized loan** requires equal periodic payments (PMT) that pay off the entire principal and interest by maturity. Each payment consists of:
1. Interest payment (based on remaining unpaid principal).
2. Principal repayment (the rest of the payment which reduces the outstanding balance).

Worked Example: Complete Amortization Table

Problem: Prepare a complete loan amortization schedule for a $10,000 loan, to be repaid in 3 equal annual installments at an interest rate of 10% per annum.

Solution:
Step 1: Calculate the annual installment payment (PMT):
Use the PVOA formula:
$10,000 = PMT \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1.10)^{-3}}{0.10} \right]$
$10,000 = PMT \times 2.48685$
$PMT = 10,000 / 2.48685 \approx \$4,021.15$ per year.

Step 2: Calculate Year-by-Year allocations:
Year 1:
- Beginning Balance = $10,000.00
- Interest expense = $10,000 \times 10\% = \$1,000.00$
- Principal Repayment = Payment - Interest = $4,021.15 - 1,000.00 = \$3,021.15$
- Ending Balance = Beginning Balance - Principal Repayment = $10,000.00 - 3,021.15 = \$6,978.85$

Year 2:
- Beginning Balance = $6,978.85
- Interest expense = $6,978.85 \times 10\% = \$697.89$
- Principal Repayment = $4,021.15 - 697.89 = \$3,323.26$
- Ending Balance = $6,978.85 - 3,323.26 = \$3,655.59$

Year 3:
- Beginning Balance = $3,655.59
- Interest expense = $3,655.59 \times 10\% = \$365.56$
- Principal Repayment = $4,021.15 - 365.56 = \$3,655.59$ (due to rounding)
- Ending Balance = $3,655.59 - 3,655.59 = \$0.00$

3.5 Uneven Cash Flows and Growing Perpetuities

In practice, business cash flows are rarely uniform annuities. For instance, a software startup may experience small initial revenues that scale rapidly:

1. Present Value of Uneven Cash Flows

To find the PV of an uneven cash flow stream, discount each cash flow individually to Year 0 and sum them:
$$PV = \sum_{t=1}^n \frac{CF_t}{(1 + r)^t}$$

Worked Example: Uneven Cash Flows

Problem: An IT project generates the following cash inflows: Year 1: $1,000, Year 2: $3,000, and Year 3: $5,000. If the discount rate is 10%, calculate the total present value today.

Solution:
- $PV(CF_1) = 1,000 / (1.10)^1 = \$909.09$
- $PV(CF_2) = 3,000 / (1.10)^2 = 3,000 / 1.21 = \$2,479.34$
- $PV(CF_3) = 5,000 / (1.10)^3 = 5,000 / 1.331 = \$3,756.57$
- Total Present Value = $909.09 + 2,479.34 + 3,756.57 = \$7,145.00$.

2. Growing Perpetuities

If a cash flow stream is expected to grow at a constant rate ($g$) forever:
$$PV = \frac{PMT}{r - g} \quad (\text{where } r > g)$$ Useful for valuing stocks with constant dividend growth.

3.6 Solving for Missing Variables ($N$ and $r$)

We can rearrange the single cash flow formula to solve for the number of periods ($N$) or the rate ($r$):

1. Solving for Interest Rate ($r$)

$$r = \left( \frac{FV_n}{PV} \right)^{\frac{1}{n}} - 1$$

Worked Example: Finding Interest Rate

Problem: You purchase a domain name for $5,000 today and sell it for $10,000 after 4 years. Calculate the annual compounded rate of return on this investment.

Solution:
$r = (10,000 / 5,000)^{1/4} - 1 = (2)^{0.25} - 1 = 1.1892 - 1 = 0.1892 \quad (18.92\%)$.
The annual compounded return is 18.92%.

2. Solving for Time Periods ($N$)

$$N = \frac{\ln(FV_n / PV)}{\ln(1 + r)}$$

Worked Example: Finding Time Periods

Problem: How long will it take for an investment of $10,000 to double to $20,000 in an account earning a 6% annual interest rate?

Solution:
$N = \frac{\ln(20,000 / 10,000)}{\ln(1 + 0.06)} = \frac{\ln(2)}{\ln(1.06)} = \frac{0.69315}{0.05827} \approx 11.90$ years.
It will take approximately 11.9 years to double.

3.7 Continuous Compounding

As compounding frequency approaches infinity ($m \to \infty$), it becomes **continuous compounding**. The formulas use Euler's constant ($e \approx 2.71828$):

  • Future Value:
    $$FV_n = PV \times e^{r \cdot n}$$
  • Present Value:
    $$PV = FV_n \times e^{-r \cdot n}$$
  • Effective Annual Rate (EAR):
    $$EAR = e^r - 1$$

Worked Example: Continuous Compounding

Problem: If you invest $1,000 at a nominal annual interest rate of 8% compounded continuously, find the future value after 5 years and the EAR.

Solution:
$FV_5 = 1,000 \times e^{0.08 \times 5} = 1,000 \times e^{0.40} = 1,000 \times 1.49182 = \$1,491.82$.
$EAR = e^{0.08} - 1 = 1.08329 - 1 = 0.08329 \quad (8.33\%)$.

3.8 Rules of Thumb and Annuity Relationship Proofs

1. The Rule of 72 and Rule of 115

For quick mental approximations, financial managers use the following rules of thumb:

  • Rule of 72 (Doubling Time):
    $$\text{Years to Double } (N) \approx \frac{72}{\text{Interest Rate } (r\%)} $$ For example, at a 6% interest rate: $N \approx 72 / 6 = 12$ years. (Exact calculation is 11.90 years, showing highly accurate approximation).
  • Rule of 115 (Tripling Time):
    $$\text{Years to Triple } (N) \approx \frac{115}{\text{Interest Rate } (r\%)} $$ For example, at a 10% interest rate: $N \approx 115 / 10 = 11.5$ years. (Exact calculation is 11.52 years).

2. Algebraic Relationship: Ordinary Annuity vs. Annuity Due

An Annuity Due differs from an **Ordinary Annuity** only because every payment is shifted backwards by one period. Thus, every single cash flow earns interest for exactly one more compounding period: 

  Ordinary Annuity Cash Flows:
  Year:    0         1         2         3         4 (Payments at end of years)
           ├─────────┼─────────┼─────────┼─────────┤
                    PMT       PMT       PMT       PMT

  Annuity Due Cash Flows:
  Year:    0         1         2         3         4 (Payments at start of years)
           ├─────────┼─────────┼─────────┼─────────┤
          PMT       PMT       PMT       PMT

Because each payment is compound-multiplied by $(1 + r)^1$ once more:
$$FV_{\text{Annuity Due}} = \sum_{t=0}^{n-1} PMT \cdot (1 + r)^{n-t} = \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{n} PMT \cdot (1 + r)^{n-t} \right] \cdot (1 + r) = FVOA_n \cdot (1 + r)$$ The same mathematical shift holds true for present values:
$$PV_{\text{Annuity Due}} = PVOA_n \cdot (1 + r)$$

3.9 Complex Financial Case Study: Multi-Stage Retirement Planning

Real-world applications of the time value of money often combine multiple stages of compounding and discounting:

Two-Stage Retirement Case Study

Scenario: A software engineer is currently 30 years old and plans to retire in 30 years (at age 60). During this accumulation phase, they plan to save $6,000 at the end of each year. The retirement account will earn an annual rate of 8%.
Upon retiring at age 60, they plan to make 20 equal annual withdrawals at the end of each year to fund their retirement. During this distribution phase, the remaining retirement pool balance will earn a safer interest rate of 5%.
Calculate: (1) The total accumulated pool at retirement age 60, and (2) The maximum annual withdrawal amount.

Solution:

Stage 1: Calculate the Future Value of Ordinary Annuity (FVOA) at age 60:
- PMT = $6,000
- interest rate $r = 8\% = 0.08$
- periods $n = 30$ years
$$FVOA_{30} = 6,000 \times \left[ \frac{(1.08)^{30} - 1}{0.08} \right]$$
$(1.08)^{30} \approx 10.06266$
$$FVOA_{30} = 6,000 \times \left[ \frac{10.06266 - 1}{0.08} \right] = 6,000 \times 113.2832 = \$679,699.20$$
The engineer accumulates **$679,699.20** by age 60.

Stage 2: Calculate the Annual Withdrawal during retirement:
The accumulated pool of $679,699.20 is now the Present Value (PVOA) of a 20-year retirement annuity:
- $PVOA = \$679,699.20$
- interest rate $r = 5\% = 0.05$
- periods $n = 20$ years
$$679,699.20 = \text{Withdrawal} \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1.05)^{-20}}{0.05} \right]$$
$(1.05)^{-20} \approx 0.37689$
$$679,699.20 = \text{Withdrawal} \times \left[ \frac{1 - 0.37689}{0.05} \right] = \text{Withdrawal} \times 12.4622$$
$$\text{Withdrawal} = \frac{679,699.20}{12.4622} \approx \$54,540.87$$
The software engineer can safely withdraw **$54,540.87** each year for 20 years during retirement.

3.10 Practical Applications: Corporate Bond Valuation and Leasing

1. Valuation of Corporate Bonds

A corporate bond is a long-term contract where a borrower agrees to make periodic interest payments (coupons) and pay a principal amount (par value) at maturity to the holder:
$$V_B = \sum_{t=1}^n \frac{\text{Coupon}_t}{(1 + r_d)^t} + \frac{\text{Par Value}_n}{(1 + r_d)^n} = \text{Coupon} \times \left[ PVOA_{r_d, n} \right] + \text{Par} \times (1 + r_d)^{-n}$$ Where $r_d$ is the required market rate of interest.

Worked Example: Bond Valuation

Problem: Calculate the value of a $1,000 par value, 10-year bond paying a 10% annual coupon ($100 coupon payment per year) when the market required rate of interest ($r_d$) is: (1) 8%, and (2) 12%.

Solution:

1. Market rate $r_d = 8\%$ (Premium Bond):
- $PV(\text{Coupons}) = 100 \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1.08)^{-10}}{0.08} \right] = 100 \times 6.71008 = \$671.01$
- $PV(\text{Principal}) = 1,000 \times (1.08)^{-10} = 1,000 \times 0.46319 = \$463.19$
- Bond Value $V_B = 671.01 + 463.19 = \$1,134.20$.
Since required rate (8%) < coupon rate (10%), the bond sells at a **premium** above par value.

2. Market rate $r_d = 12\%$ (Discount Bond):
- $PV(\text{Coupons}) = 100 \times \left[ \frac{1 - (1.12)^{-10}}{0.12} \right] = 100 \times 5.65022 = \$565.02$
- $PV(\text{Principal}) = 1,000 \times (1.12)^{-10} = 1,000 \times 0.32197 = \$321.97$
- Bond Value $V_B = 565.02 + 321.97 = \$886.99$.
Since required rate (12%) > coupon rate (10%), the bond sells at a **discount** below par value.

2. Lease vs. Buy Decisions

Firms use present value techniques to evaluate whether to acquire assets through leasing or purchase. Lease payments are tax-deductible expenses (operating lease), whereas buying allows the firm to claim tax deductions for depreciation and interest expense. The firm discounts the after-tax cash flows of both options at its after-tax cost of debt and chooses the option with the lower present value of cash outflows.

3.11 Valuation of Growing Annuities

A **Growing Annuity** is a stream of cash flows that grows at a constant rate ($g$) for a finite number of periods ($n$):
$$PV = \frac{PMT}{r - g} \times \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{1 + g}{1 + r} \right)^n \right] \quad (\text{where } r \neq g)$$ Where $PMT$ is the initial payment at the end of Year 1.

Growing Annuity Worked Example

Problem: A firm signs a 5-year lease contract for office space. The payment for Year 1 is $10,000, and payments will increase by 4% each year thereafter (growth rate $g = 4\%$). If the appropriate discount rate is 8%, find the present value of the lease lease obligations.

Solution:
- $PMT = \$10,000$
- discount rate $r = 8\% = 0.08$
- growth rate $g = 4\% = 0.04$
- years $n = 5$
$$PV = \frac{10,000}{0.08 - 0.04} \times \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{1.04}{1.08} \right)^5 \right]$$
$$PV = \frac{10,000}{0.04} \times \left[ 1 - (0.96296)^5 \right]$$
$$PV = 250,000 \times [ 1 - 0.8284 ] = 250,000 \times 0.1716 = \$42,900$$
The present value of the lease obligations is $42,900.

Key Applications:
1. *Pension Plans:* Valuation of retirement packages that grow annually to keep pace with inflation (COLA).
2. *Corporate Dividends:* Valuing stocks whose dividends grow at a constant rate over a finite horizon.
3. *Multi-Year Contracts:* Valuing corporate partnerships or player contracts in sports which feature yearly salary escalations.

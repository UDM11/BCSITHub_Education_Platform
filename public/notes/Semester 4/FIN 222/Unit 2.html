Unit 2: Financial Statement Analysis [10 Hrs.]

2.1 Core Financial Statements

Financial statements are the structured reports prepared by accountants to communicate the financial condition and performance of a firm. The three main financial statements are:

1. The Balance Sheet

Provides a snapshot of a firm's financial position at a **specific point in time** (e.g., December 31, 2026). It is governed by the fundamental accounting equation:
$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Shareholders' Equity}$$

  • Assets: Resources owned by the firm (Current Assets: cash, accounts receivable, inventory; Fixed Assets: property, plant, equipment, software assets).
  • Liabilities: Obligations/debts owed to creditors (Current Liabilities: accounts payable, accrued wages, short-term debt; Long-term Liabilities: long-term bank loans, bonds).
  • Shareholders' Equity: The owners' residual claim on the assets after paying off liabilities. Includes common stock, retained earnings, and paid-in capital.

2. The Income Statement (Profit or Loss Statement)

Summarizes the firm's revenues, expenses, and profits over a **period of time** (e.g., for the year ended December 31, 2026). 

  Income Statement Flow:

     Net Sales (Revenues)
  -  Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
  ───────────────────────────────────────
  =  Gross Profit
  -  Operating Expenses (SG&A, R&D, Depreciation)
  ───────────────────────────────────────
  =  Operating Income (EBIT - Earnings Before Interest & Taxes)
  -  Interest Expense
  ───────────────────────────────────────
  =  Earnings Before Taxes (EBT)
  -  Corporate Income Taxes
  ───────────────────────────────────────
  =  Net Income (Earnings available to common shareholders)

3. The Cash Flow Statement

Reports the actual cash inflows and outflows of a firm over a **period of time**, categorizing them into three main activities:

  1. Operating Activities: Cash flows generated from day-to-day core business operations (includes net income, adding back non-cash depreciation, adjusting for changes in working capital like inventory and accounts receivable).
  2. Investing Activities: Cash flows related to the purchase or sale of long-term assets (capital expenditures, acquisition of other firms, purchase of securities).
  3. Financing Activities: Cash flows related to raising capital and paying back investors (issuing stock, borrowing debt, paying dividends, repurchasing shares).

2.2 Nature and Need of Financial Ratio Analysis

What is a Financial Ratio?

A **financial ratio** is a mathematical relationship between two or more figures found in the financial statements. It standardizes financial information, allowing comparison across firms of different sizes and across time.

Why do we need Ratio Analysis?

  • Performance Evaluation: Helps managers, investors, and creditors assess firm efficiency, profitability, and risk.
  • Comparison:
    • Cross-Sectional Analysis: Comparing a firm's ratios with competitors or industry averages at a single point in time.
    • Trend (Time-Series) Analysis: Evaluating changes in ratios over several years to identify patterns.
  • Planning and Forecasting: Helps managers project future cash flows and plan capital structure.
  • Credit Evaluation: Banks use liquidity and debt ratios to assess if a firm can pay back loans.

Limitations of Ratio Analysis

  • Different accounting policies (e.g., LIFO vs FIFO for inventory, straight-line vs accelerated depreciation) make comparisons difficult.
  • Seasonal factors can distort ratios (e.g., inventory ratios for retail businesses change drastically before/after holidays).
  • "Window Dressing" — companies can temporarily manipulate year-end balance sheets to make ratios look better.
  • Inflation can distort historical asset values.
  • Ratios show *what* happened, not *why* it happened. They are indicators, not diagnostic solutions.

2.3 Types of Financial Ratios and Formulas

1. Liquidity Ratios

Measure a firm's ability to meet its short-term debt obligations as they fall due.

  • Current Ratio:
    $$\text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Current Assets}}{\text{Total Current Liabilities}}$$ Standard target: 2.0.
  • Quick (Acid-Test) Ratio: Inventory is excluded because it is the least liquid current asset.
    $$\text{Quick Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets} - \text{Inventory}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}$$ Standard target: 1.0.

2. Asset Management (Activity) Ratios

Measure how effectively a firm is managing its assets to generate sales.

  • Inventory Turnover Ratio: How many times inventory is sold and replaced in a year.
    $$\text{Inventory Turnover} = \frac{\text{Cost of Goods Sold}}{\text{Inventory}}$$
  • Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) / Average Collection Period: Average days taken to collect cash from credit customers.
    $$\text{DSO} = \frac{\text{Accounts Receivable}}{\text{Annual Credit Sales} / 365}$$
  • Total Assets Turnover: Sales generated per dollar of assets.
    $$\text{Total Assets Turnover} = \frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$

3. Debt Management (Leverage) Ratios

Measure the degree to which a firm is financed by debt and its ability to service that debt.

  • Debt Ratio (Total Debt to Total Assets):
    $$\text{Debt Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$
  • Debt-to-Equity Ratio:
    $$\text{Debt-to-Equity} = \frac{\text{Total Liabilities}}{\text{Total Shareholders' Equity}}$$
  • Times Interest Earned (TIE) Ratio: Ability to meet annual interest payments.
    $$\text{TIE} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest Expense}}$$

4. Profitability Ratios

Measure the firm's capacity to generate earnings relative to sales, assets, and equity.

  • Gross Profit Margin:
    $$\text{Gross Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Net Sales}}$$
  • Net Profit Margin:
    $$\text{Net Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Net Sales}}$$
  • Return on Assets (ROA):
    $$\text{ROA} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$
  • Return on Equity (ROE): Measures return for common shareholders.
    $$\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Common Shareholders' Equity}}$$

5. Market Value Ratios

Relate the firm's stock price to its earnings and book value.

  • Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio: Indicates how much investors are willing to pay per dollar of current profits.
    $$\text{P/E Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Price per Share}}{\text{Earnings per Share (EPS)}}$$
  • Market-to-Book (M/B) Ratio:
    $$\text{M/B Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Price per Share}}{\text{Book Value per Share}}$$

2.4 The DuPont Analysis System and Numerical Cases

The DuPont Equation

The **DuPont Equation** decomposes Return on Equity (ROE) into three components: profit margin (representing expense control), asset turnover (representing asset efficiency), and the equity multiplier (representing leverage): 

  ROE = Profit Margin × Total Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier

  ROE = (Net Income / Sales) × (Sales / Total Assets) × (Total Assets / Equity)

This decomposition helps management pinpoint the exact reason why ROE is high or low. For example, a firm can achieve high ROE through high profit margins (like premium luxury goods), high asset turnover (like grocery stores), or high leverage (which increases risk).

Ratio Calculation Worked Numerical

Problem: Given the following financial data for Nepalese Tech Solutions Ltd:
- Cash = $10,000; Accounts Receivable = $20,000; Inventory = $30,000; Net Fixed Assets = $140,000
- Accounts Payable = $15,000; Notes Payable = $5,000; Long-term Debt = $60,000
- Net Sales = $300,000; Cost of Goods Sold = $180,000; Interest Expense = $8,000; Net Income = $24,000
Calculate: (1) Current Ratio, (2) Quick Ratio, (3) Total Assets Turnover, (4) Debt-to-Equity Ratio, (5) ROE using the DuPont Equation.

Solution:

1. Compute Totals:
Total Current Assets = Cash + A/R + Inventory = $10,000 + 20,000 + 30,000 = $60,000.
Total Assets = Current Assets + Net Fixed Assets = $60,000 + 140,000 = $200,000.
Total Current Liabilities = Accounts Payable + Notes Payable = $15,000 + 5,000 = $20,000.
Total Liabilities = Current Liabilities + Long-term Debt = $20,000 + 60,000 = $80,000.
Common Shareholders' Equity = Total Assets - Total Liabilities = $200,000 - 80,000 = $120,000.

2. Calculate Ratios:
(1) $\text{Current Ratio} = \text{Current Assets} / \text{Current Liabilities} = 60,000 / 20,000 = 3.0$.
(2) $\text{Quick Ratio} = (\text{Current Assets} - \text{Inventory}) / \text{Current Liabilities} = (60,000 - 30,000) / 20,000 = 1.5$.
(3) $\text{Total Assets Turnover} = \text{Sales} / \text{Total Assets} = 300,000 / 200,000 = 1.5$ times.
(4) $\text{Debt-to-Equity Ratio} = \text{Total Liabilities} / \text{Equity} = 80,000 / 120,000 \approx 0.67 \quad (67\%)$.

3. DuPont ROE Analysis:
- Profit Margin = $\text{Net Income} / \text{Sales} = 24,000 / 300,000 = 0.08 \quad (8\%)$.
- Total Asset Turnover = 1.5.
- Equity Multiplier = $\text{Total Assets} / \text{Equity} = 200,000 / 120,000 \approx 1.667$.
$\text{ROE} = 8\% \times 1.5 \times 1.667 \approx 12\% \times 1.667 \approx 20\%$.
Verify directly: $\text{ROE} = \text{Net Income} / \text{Equity} = 24,000 / 120,000 = 0.20 = 20\%$.

2.5 Common-Size Financial Statements

Standardizing financial statements by expressing each line item as a percentage of a common base is called Common-Size Analysis. This assists managers in spotting structural shifts over time:

  • Common-Size Income Statement: Every expense and profit margin line item is divided by **Net Sales**:
    $$\text{Common Size \%} = \frac{\text{Line Item Value}}{\text{Net Sales}} \times 100$$ Allows easy identification of whether operating costs, R&D expenses, or SG&A are growing disproportionately compared to revenues.
  • Common-Size Balance Sheet: Every asset, liability, and equity account is divided by **Total Assets**:
    $$\text{Common Size \%} = \frac{\text{Line Item Value}}{\text{Total Assets}} \times 100$$ Shows the asset mix (e.g., what percentage of assets are tied up in cash or inventory) and the capital structure mix.

2.6 Window Dressing and Creative Accounting Tactics

Companies sometimes engage in **window dressing** — legal but misleading actions to make their financial statements look better at the end of the fiscal year:

  • Artificially Boosting Liquidity (Current Ratio):
    Assume current assets = $100,000 and current liabilities = $50,000. Current ratio = 2.0.
    If the firm pays off $20,000 of accounts payable just before the year-end:
    New current assets = $80,000; New current liabilities = $30,000.
    New Current Ratio = $80,000 / $30,000 = 2.67.
    The current ratio appears stronger, even though no new cash entered the business.
  • Accelerating Revenue Recognition: Shipping goods early or booking service revenues before the work is completed to inflate the current year's earnings.
  • Delaying Expenses: Postponing maintenance or advertising expenses to the next fiscal year.

2.7 DuPont System Comparison: Margin vs. Turnover Focus

Consider two companies with the exact same Return on Equity (ROE) of 15% operating under different models:

Company Profit Margin (PM) Total Asset Turnover (TAT) Equity Multiplier (EM) Calculation Result (ROE)
PremiumTech (Software) 15% 0.5 2.0 $15\% \times 0.5 \times 2.0$ 15%
BulkRetail (Hardware) 3% 2.5 2.0 $3\% \times 2.5 \times 2.0$ 15%

- **PremiumTech** generates high profit margins per sale (15%) but has low asset turnover (0.5), meaning it doesn't utilize its assets rapidly. This is typical of high-margin SaaS/Software platforms.
- **BulkRetail** generates low profit margins (3%) but turns over its assets very rapidly (2.5 times a year). This is typical of hardware distributors or grocery stores.
- Both achieve the same ROE, demonstrating that DuPont analysis uncovers the underlying drivers of financial performance.

2.8 Case Study: Nepalese Public Company Disclosures

In Nepal, public limited companies (such as commercial banks, manufacturing giants like Unilever Nepal or Shivam Cement, and hydropower operators) are required to publish condensed quarterly financial disclosures in major newspapers and submit complete annual audited statements to the Securities Board of Nepal (SEBON) and **Nepal Stock Exchange (NEPSE)**.

Quarterly Report Analysis Checklist

  • Section 1: Balance Sheet Structure: Nepalese balance sheets list Share Capital, Reserves, and Long-Term/Short-Term Borrowings first on the Liabilities side, followed by Fixed Assets, Investments, and Current Assets.
  • Section 2: Profit or Loss Highlights: Major elements include Operating Revenue (Sales), Cost of Sales, Administrative Expenses, Finance Costs (Interest), and Net Profit.
  • Section 3: Key Financial Indicators: Usually printed at the bottom of the newspaper advertisement, listing:
    • Earnings Per Share (EPS)
    • Net Worth Per Share
    • Liquidity Ratio (Current Ratio)
    • Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio

2.9 Free Cash Flow (FCF) Analysis

While Net Income is an accounting profit, investors focus on **Free Cash Flow (FCF)**, which is the cash actually available to distribute to all investors (debt holders and equity holders) after paying taxes and making necessary capital expenditures and working capital investments:

Free Cash Flow Formula

$$FCF = \left[ EBIT(1 - T) + \text{Depreciation} \right] - \left[ \text{Capital Expenditures} + \Delta \text{Net Operating Working Capital} \right]$$ Where:
- $EBIT(1 - T)$ is Net Operating Profit After Taxes (NOPAT).
- Depreciation is added back because it is a non-cash expense.
- Capital Expenditures (CapEx) is cash spent to buy/maintain physical fixed assets.
- $\Delta$ Net Operating Working Capital (NOWC) is the change in current assets needed for operations minus accounts payable.

FCF Worked Numerical

Problem: In 2026, Tech Nepal Pvt Ltd reported:
- EBIT = $150,000; Tax Rate T = 30%
- Depreciation Expense = $20,000
- Capital Expenditures (purchase of fixed servers/laptops) = $45,000
- Operating Working Capital grew from $50,000 (2025) to $60,000 (2026) ($\Delta NOWC = \$10,000$).
Calculate the firm's Free Cash Flow.

Solution:
1. Calculate NOPAT:
$\text{NOPAT} = EBIT(1 - T) = 150,000 \times (1 - 0.30) = 150,000 \times 0.70 = \$105,000$.

2. Calculate Cash Flow from Operations before CapEx:
$\text{Operating Cash Flow} = NOPAT + \text{Depreciation} = 105,000 + 20,000 = \$125,000$.

3. Compute FCF:
$FCF = 125,000 - (\text{CapEx} + \Delta NOWC) = 125,000 - (45,000 + 10,000) = 125,000 - 55,000 = \$70,000$.
The firm generated $70,000 of free cash flow during the year, which can be used to pay dividends or pay off bank loans.

2.10 Ratio Benchmarks and Liquidity Trade-offs

Ratios cannot be analyzed in isolation. They must be compared against standard industry benchmarks:

Ratio Type Standard Benchmark If Too Low (Implications) If Too High (Implications)
Current Ratio 1.5 to 2.0 Liquidity risk; high chance of defaulting on short-term debts. Inefficient capital use; excess cash or idle inventory yielding 0% return.
Inventory Turnover 5.0 to 8.0 times Slow-moving, obsolete stock; cash tied up in inventories. Frequent stock-outs, lost sales, ordering costs are too high.
Times Interest Earned > 3.0 times Debt distress; risk of lenders foreclosing on corporate assets. Under-utilization of debt tax shield benefits.

The Liquidity-Profitability Trade-off

Financial managers face a constant trade-off between liquidity and profitability:
1. **High Liquidity:** Protects the firm from bankruptcy, but reduces profitability because cash and liquid assets earn little to no returns.
2. **Low Liquidity:** Maximizes profitability by investing all cash into high-yielding project assets, but increases insolvency risk.

2.11 Advanced Debt Service Metrics and Users of Ratio Analysis

1. EBITDA Coverage Ratio

While the Times Interest Earned (TIE) ratio measures a firm's ability to pay interest, it has two drawbacks: it ignores principal repayments, and it uses EBIT (which subtracts non-cash depreciation). The **EBITDA Coverage Ratio** resolves this:
$$\text{EBITDA Coverage} = \frac{\text{EBITDA} + \text{Lease Payments}}{\text{Interest} + \text{Lease Payments} + \frac{\text{Principal Repayments}}{1 - T}}$$ Where EBITDA is EBIT + Depreciation + Amortization.

2. Who Uses Financial Ratios?

  • Trade Creditors / Suppliers: Focus on **Liquidity Ratios** (Current, Quick) to ensure the firm can pay for supplies purchased on credit within 30-90 days.
  • Commercial Banks / Long-term Lenders: Focus on **Leverage & Debt Coverage Ratios** (Debt-to-Equity, TIE, EBITDA Coverage) to evaluate if the firm's long-term cash flow is stable enough to pay annual interest and principal repayments.
  • Shareholders & Stock Analysts: Focus on **Profitability and Market Ratios** (ROE, Profit Margin, P/E, EPS) to assess future stock price appreciation and dividend growth potential.
  • Internal Management: Uses all ratios, especially **Asset Management Ratios** (Inventory Turnover, DSO), to identify operational bottlenecks and improve production efficiency.

2.12 Industry Benchmark Standards: SIC and NAICS Codes

To conduct meaningful cross-sectional analyses, firms must compare themselves against true peers. Financial analysts use standardized classification systems:

  • SIC (Standard Industrial Classification) Codes: A four-digit code system used by the US government to classify industries.
  • NAICS (North American Industry Classification System) Codes: A newer, more detailed six-digit system that replaced SIC, providing finer categories for information technology, software services, and e-commerce sectors.
  • Application: Databases like Bloomberg or Reuters use these codes to aggregate sector financial data, generating the industry average benchmarks that firms use to gauge their performance.

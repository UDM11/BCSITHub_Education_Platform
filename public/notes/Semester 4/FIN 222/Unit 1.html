1.1 Concept of Financial Management and Finance within the Organization

What is Financial Management?

Financial management refers to the efficient and effective planning, organizing, directing, and controlling of financial activities in an organization. It involves the acquisition of funds, allocation of funds (investment), and distribution of returns (dividends) to maximize the value of the firm for its shareholders.

In simple terms, financial management is concerned with:

  • Where to get the money (Financing Decision)
  • Where to put the money (Investment Decision)
  • How to share the profits (Dividend Decision)
  • How to manage daily cash flows (Working Capital Decision)

Finance within the Organizational Structure

In a typical corporation, the finance department is headed by the Chief Financial Officer (CFO), who reports directly to the Chief Executive Officer (CEO). Under the CFO, the department is split into two main operational paths:

1.2 Three Major Financial Management Decisions (Functions)

The core functions of financial management revolve around three major strategic decisions:

1. The Investment Decision (Capital Budgeting)

Concerned with the selection of assets in which funds will be invested by the firm. The firm must evaluate projects that yield future benefits exceeding their initial costs:

  • Long-term Investment: Purchasing land, buildings, machinery, or developing software systems (Capital Budgeting).
  • Short-term Investment: Managing current assets like inventory, cash, and receivables (Working Capital Management).

2. The Financing Decision

Concerned with determining the best mix of sources of funds (Debt vs. Equity) to finance the long-term investments. This is called the Capital Structure decision:

  • Equity Capital: Funds raised from shareholders (issuing common/preferred stock, retaining earnings). No obligation to repay, but shares ownership.
  • Debt Capital: Funds borrowed from banks or raised by issuing bonds/debentures. Fixed interest obligation, must be repaid, but interest is tax-deductible.

3. The Dividend Decision

Concerned with deciding how much of the firm's net profit should be distributed as dividends to shareholders, and how much should be retained in the business (retained earnings) for future growth.

1.3 The Financial Goal: Profit vs. Wealth Maximization

A firm must have a clear objective to guide its financial decisions. The two primary schools of thought are:

1. Profit Maximization

Argues that the primary objective of a firm is to maximize its total net profits (earnings per share - EPS) during a given period.

  • Arguments for: Simple to measure, indicates economic efficiency, direct connection to survival.
  • Drawbacks (Why it is rejected as a primary goal):
    • Ambiguity: What kind of profit? (Short-term, long-term, gross profit, net profit, before-tax, after-tax?).
    • Ignores Time Value of Money: Treats cash flows received in Year 1 and Year 5 equally.
    • Ignores Risk: A highly risky project that might fail may show higher projected profit than a safe project.
    • Ignores Quality of Cash Flows: Focuses on accounting profits (accrual) rather than cash in hand.

2. Wealth Maximization (Shareholder Value Maximization)

Argues that the primary objective of a firm is to maximize the market value of its common stock (share price). Value is determined by the timing, size, and risk associated with projected cash flows.

  • Arguments for:
    • Considers the **Time Value of Money** (discounts future cash flows to present value).
    • Considers the **Risk/Uncertainty** (higher risk projects require higher discount rates, reducing present value).
    • Focuses on **Cash Flows** rather than accounting profits.
    • Directly aligns with the financial interests of the owners (shareholders).

1.4 The Agency Problem and Corporate Governance

What is the Agency Relationship?

An agency relationship exists when one party (the principal) hires another party (the agent) to perform a service and delegates decision-making authority to them. In corporate finance, the key relationships are:
$$\text{Shareholders (Owners / Principals)} \longleftrightarrow \text{Managers (Agents)}$$ $$\text{Shareholders (Owners / Principals)} \longleftrightarrow \text{Creditors (Debt Holders)}$$

The Agency Problem (Conflict of Interest)

Since managers do not own 100% of the firm, a conflict of interest can arise. Managers may prioritize their own personal goals (higher salaries, job security, power, corporate jets) over the goal of wealth maximization for the shareholders. This conflict leads to **Agency Costs** (monitoring costs, auditing costs, bonding costs, and opportunity costs of lost decisions).

Methods to Resolve Manager-Shareholder Conflict

  1. Managerial Compensation Plans: Aligning managers' interests with shareholders by offering:
    • Performance shares (linked to stock price increases).
    • Stock options (option to buy shares at a fixed price in the future).
    • Executive bonuses tied to long-term profitability.
  2. Direct Intervention by Shareholders: Large institutional shareholders (pension funds, mutual funds) using voting power to influence management decisions.
  3. The Threat of Firing: Shareholders voting at annual meetings to replace ineffective managers.
  4. The Threat of Takeovers: Underperforming firms with low stock prices are targets for hostile takeovers. If taken over, management is usually fired. This keeps managers motivated to keep share prices high.

Corporate Governance

Corporate Governance is the system of rules, practices, and processes by which a firm is directed and controlled. It provides the framework for balancing the interests of a company's many stakeholders (shareholders, management, customers, suppliers, financiers, government, and the community).

1.5 Forms of Business Organization and Ethics in Finance

Forms of Business Organization

There are three primary forms of business organization, each with its own financial characteristics:

Form Description Advantages Disadvantages
Sole Proprietorship Owned and run by a single individual. Easy and cheap to set up, minimal regulation, owner keeps all profits. **Unlimited personal liability**, limited capital, limited life of business.
Partnership Owned by two or more individuals. Easy to form, combined skills and capital, tax benefits. **Unlimited personal liability** (joint and several), partnership transfer difficulty, conflict risk.
Corporation A legal entity separate from its owners (shareholders). **Limited liability**, easy transfer of ownership, unlimited life, high capital-raising capacity. **Double taxation** (corporate profit + individual dividends), high setup costs, strict government regulation.

Ethics in Finance

Ethics refers to a set of moral principles and values. Ethical financial behavior involves:

  • Ensuring fair, complete, and accurate financial reporting.
  • Avoiding conflicts of interest (insider trading, self-dealing).
  • Protecting customer and employee financial data.
  • Avoiding misleading financial projections or product promises.
  • Adhering strictly to tax regulations and local financial laws.

Enron/WorldCom Lesson: Unethical behavior (inflating revenues, hiding debt) might yield temporary profit spikes but ultimately leads to corporate bankruptcy, loss of investor confidence, and severe criminal penalties.

1.6 Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) and the Triple Bottom Line

Modern corporate finance has moved beyond focusing solely on shareholder wealth. Firms are increasingly evaluated on **Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)**:

  • The Triple Bottom Line Framework: Argues that companies should measure their success using three performance metrics:
    • People (Social): Fair wages, safe working environments, community engagement, and labor rights.
    • Planet (Environmental): Carbon footprint reduction, waste management, sustainable sourcing, and ecological conservation.
    • Profit (Financial): The traditional economic value created (wealth maximization).
  • ESG Criteria (Environmental, Social, Governance): Investors use these three factors to screen potential investments, rewarding companies that protect the environment and maintain strong governance standards.

1.7 The Financial Environment: Markets and Intermediaries

For a firm to raise capital effectively, it must operate within the financial system:

1. Financial Markets

Venues where buyers and sellers trade financial assets (stocks, bonds, currencies):

  • Money Markets vs. Capital Markets:
    • Money Markets: Where short-term debt securities (maturity of less than 1 year) are traded (e.g., T-bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit). Highly liquid, low risk.
    • Capital Markets: Where long-term debt and equity securities (maturity of 1 year or more) are traded (e.g., common stock, corporate bonds). Higher risk and return profiles.
  • Primary Markets vs. Secondary Markets:
    • Primary Markets: Where corporations raise new capital by issuing securities for the first time (e.g., an Initial Public Offering - IPO). The proceeds go directly to the issuing firm.
    • Secondary Markets: Where existing outstanding securities are traded among investors (e.g., buying and selling shares on the stock exchange like NEPSE). The issuing company receives no money.

2. Financial Intermediaries

Institutions that bridge the gap between savers (lenders) and spenders (borrowers):

  • Commercial Banks: Accept deposits and lend money directly to businesses.
  • Investment Banks: Help corporations issue securities (underwriting IPOs) and advise on mergers.
  • Mutual Funds: Pool funds from many individual investors to buy diversified portfolios of stocks and bonds.

1.8 Financial Dynamics in the IT and Software Industry

Financial management in IT and software firms differs significantly from traditional manufacturing:

  • High Intangible Assets: IT firms hold intellectual property, patents, and software source code rather than heavy factory machinery. This makes getting traditional bank loans difficult because banks prefer tangible collateral.
  • Reliance on Venture Capital (VC): Tech startups often run at a loss for years while building software platforms. They rely on VC equity financing round-by-round (Seed, Series A, Series B) rather than borrowing debt.
  • High R&D Expenditures: A massive percentage of revenues is reinvested in research, development, and engineering salaries, which are expensed immediately, depressing short-term accounting profits.

1.9 Shareholder-Creditor Conflicts (Agency Conflict II)

While shareholders elect managers to maximize stock prices, conflicts can occur between shareholders and creditors (lenders/bondholders):

  • Asset Substitution: Lenders lend money based on the risk of the firm's current assets. If shareholders approve switching to a high-risk project, the value of the debt drops because default risk increases. If the project succeeds, shareholders keep all upside; if it fails, creditors absorb the loss.
  • Debt Overhang (Underinvestment): If a firm is close to default, shareholders may reject profitable projects because the returns will go to pay off creditors rather than benefit shareholders.
  • Protective Covenants: Creditors protect themselves by writing legal restrictions into loan agreements (e.g., limiting additional debt, requiring minimum current ratios, restricting dividend payouts).

1.10 Institutional Corporate Governance and Regulatory Frameworks

Effective corporate governance relies on structured boards and strict regulatory oversight to prevent corporate fraud:

  • The Board of Directors (BOD): Elected by shareholders to oversee management.
    • Inside Directors: Employees of the firm (e.g., CEO, CFO) who understand internal operations.
    • Independent Outside Directors: Non-employees who provide unbiased oversight.
    • Audit Committee: Must consist entirely of independent directors. Oversees internal audits and hires external accountants.
    • Compensation Committee: Designs pay packages for executives to ensure alignment with shareholder interests.
  • Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX) Act: A regulatory act introduced after major accounting scandals (Enron, WorldCom) to protect investors by improving corporate disclosures:
    • Requires the CEO and CFO to personally certify that financial reports are accurate.
    • Imposes harsh criminal penalties for destroying financial records or retaliating against whistleblowers.
    • Requires external auditors to review and certify the firm's internal financial control systems.

1.11 Advanced Value Metrics: EVA and MVA

To evaluate whether management is truly creating shareholder value, modern firms use two metrics:

1. Economic Value Added (EVA)

Measures the true economic profit of a firm by subtracting the dollar cost of all capital used (both debt and equity) from Net Operating Profit After Taxes (NOPAT):
$$EVA = NOPAT - (\text{Capital Invested} \times WACC)$$ If $EVA$ is positive, management has created wealth; if negative, they have destroyed capital even if the accounting profit is positive.

2. Market Value Added (MVA)

Measures the cumulative wealth created for shareholders since the firm's inception:
$$MVA = \text{Market Value of Equity} - \text{Book Value of Equity}$$ Where Market Value is (Share Price $\times$ Number of Outstanding Shares), and Book Value is Common Equity as reported on the Balance Sheet.

1.12 The IT Startup Capital Accumulation Cycle

Unlike traditional brick-and-mortar firms, IT startups raise capital in sequential phases: 

  Bootstrapping ──► Angel Investors ──► Venture Capital (VC) ──► IPO (NEPSE/NYSE)
  (Own savings)      (High Net Worth)     (Series A, B, C...)     (Public Markets)
  1. Bootstrapping: Founders fund the startup using personal savings or credit cards.
  2. Angel Investors: Wealthy individuals who provide initial seed capital in exchange for convertible debt or ownership equity.
  3. Venture Capital: Institutional investment firms that invest large sums (millions of dollars) in high-growth startups, participating actively in board decisions and strategic directions.
  4. Initial Public Offering (IPO): The transition from a private company to a public corporation by selling shares to the general public, allowing early investors to exit (cash out).

1.13 Core Differences: Finance vs. Accounting

While both disciplines deal with the management of money, they operate under different principles:

Aspect Accounting Finance
Core Standard Accrual Method (revenues/expenses are recognized when they occur, not when cash changes hands). Cash Flow Method (focuses strictly on actual cash inflows and outflows).
Primary Objective Collect and present financial data to reflect the firm's historical performance. Plan, allocate, and acquire capital to maximize future shareholder wealth.
Decision Making Reports on past transactions. Does not actively direct future investments. Active decision making using future cash flow projections and risk assessments.

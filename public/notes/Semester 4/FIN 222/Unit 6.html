Unit 6: Basics of Capital Budgeting Decisions [9 Hrs.]

6.1 Concept and Significance of Capital Budgeting

What is Capital Budgeting?

Capital Budgeting is the process of planning and evaluating long-term capital expenditures (investments in fixed assets like machinery, research and development projects, or new IT systems) whose cash flows are expected to extend beyond one year.

Why is Capital Budgeting Critical (Significance)?

  • Long-term Effects: Capital decisions shape the firm's cost structure, production capabilities, and competitiveness for years to come.
  • Large Resource Commitments: Capital projects require massive cash layouts that can bankrupt a firm if they fail.
  • Irreversibility: Once fixed assets are purchased or custom software is developed, they have little to no salvage value. Undoing a bad decision causes huge financial losses.

Classification of Capital Projects

  • Independent Projects: Projects whose cash flows are completely unrelated. Accepting or rejecting one project does not affect the decision on the other project.
  • Mutually Exclusive Projects: Projects that perform the same function. If we accept one, we must reject the other (e.g., building a bridge vs. running a ferry service).
  • Replacement Projects: Upgrading worn-out or obsolete equipment to reduce operating costs.
  • Expansion Projects: Entering new markets or launching new product lines to increase sales.

6.2 Capital Budgeting Evaluation Techniques

Firms use multiple techniques to evaluate projects, split into non-discounting (traditional) and discounting (modern) methods:

1. Payback Period (PBP)

The length of time required for an investment to recover its initial cost from its cash inflows.

  • Formula (Equal cash flows):
    $$\text{PBP} = \frac{\text{Initial Investment}}{\text{Annual Cash Inflow}}$$
  • Unequal cash flows: Sum the cash inflows year by year until they equal the initial investment.
    $$\text{PBP} = A + \frac{B}{C}$$ Where $A$ is the last year with a negative cumulative cash flow, $B$ is the remaining unpaid cost, and $C$ is the cash flow in the following year.
  • Decision Rule: Accept if $PBP \le \text{Target Payback Period}$.

2. Discounted Payback Period (DPBP)

Similar to payback, but uses discounted present values of cash flows rather than nominal ones, accounting for TVM.

3. Net Present Value (NPV)

The sum of the present values of all cash inflows minus the initial cost. Considered the gold standard of capital budgeting.

$$NPV = \sum_{t=1}^n \frac{CF_t}{(1 + k)^t} - CF_0$$ Where $CF_t$ is cash flow in year $t$, $k$ is cost of capital (WACC), and $CF_0$ is initial investment.
Decision Rule:
- Accept if $NPV > 0$ (Increases shareholder wealth).
- Reject if $NPV < 0$.

4. Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

The discount rate that forces the NPV of a project to equal exactly zero.

$$\sum_{t=1}^n \frac{CF_t}{(1 + IRR)^t} - CF_0 = 0$$ Calculated by trial-and-error and interpolation:
$$IRR = LDR + \left[ \frac{NPV_{LDR}}{NPV_{LDR} - NPV_{HDR}} \right] \times (HDR - LDR)$$ Where LDR is Lower Discount Rate and HDR is Higher Discount Rate.
Decision Rule: Accept if $IRR \ge k$ (Cost of Capital).

5. Profitability Index (PI)

The ratio of the present value of future cash inflows to the initial cost (measures benefit-cost ratio):

$$PI = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^n \frac{CF_t}{(1 + k)^t}}{CF_0}$$ Decision Rule: Accept if $PI \ge 1.0$.

6.3 NPV vs. IRR Conflicts

For **independent projects**, NPV and IRR always yield the same accept/reject decisions. However, for **mutually exclusive projects**, conflicts can arise (NPV selects Project A, but IRR selects Project B):

  • Causes of Conflicts:
    • Scale Difference: One project is much larger than the other.
    • Timing Difference: Cash flows of one project arrive early; the other arrives late.
    • Reinvestment Rate Assumption: NPV assumes cash flows are reinvested at the **Cost of Capital ($k$)**, while IRR assumes cash flows are reinvested at the **project's own IRR**. The NPV assumption is more realistic.
  • Resolution: Always follow the **NPV rule** because it directly measures wealth maximization.

6.4 Worked Numerical Example

Capital Budgeting Calculations

Problem: Nepalese Software Hub is evaluating a new software development project with the following cash flows:
- Initial Investment $CF_0 = \$50,000$
- Year 1 Cash Inflow $CF_1 = \$20,000$
- Year 2 Cash Inflow $CF_2 = \$25,000$
- Year 3 Cash Inflow $CF_3 = \$30,000$
The firm's cost of capital (WACC) is 10%.
Calculate: (1) Payback Period, (2) Net Present Value (NPV), (3) Profitability Index (PI), and (4) Internal Rate of Return (IRR).

Solution:

1. Payback Period (PBP):
- Year 0: -$50,000 remaining.
- Year 1: -$30,000 remaining (after receiving $20,000).
- Year 2: -$5,000 remaining (after receiving $25,000).
- Year 3: Cash inflow is $30,000.
$\text{PBP} = 2 + (5,000 / 30,000) = 2.17$ years.

2. Net Present Value (NPV):
Discount the cash flows at 10%:
- $PV(CF_1) = 20,000 / (1.10)^1 = \$18,181.82$
- $PV(CF_2) = 25,000 / (1.10)^2 = 25,000 / 1.21 = \$20,661.16$
- $PV(CF_3) = 30,000 / (1.10)^3 = 30,000 / 1.331 = \$22,539.44$
Sum of PV of Inflows = $18,181.82 + 20,661.16 + 22,539.44 = \$61,382.42$.
$\text{NPV} = \text{Sum of PV of Inflows} - CF_0 = 61,382.42 - 50,000 = \$11,382.42$.
Since $NPV > 0$, the project is acceptable.

3. Profitability Index (PI):
$\text{PI} = \text{Sum of PV of Inflows} / CF_0 = 61,382.42 / 50,000 = 1.228$.
Since $PI > 1.0$, the project is acceptable.

4. Internal Rate of Return (IRR):
We need to find the rate where $NPV = 0$.
At $k = 10\%$, $NPV = +\$11,382.42$.
Let's try a higher discount rate, say $k = 25\%$:
- $PV(CF_1) = 20,000 / (1.25)^1 = \$16,000.00$
- $PV(CF_2) = 25,000 / (1.25)^2 = \$16,000.00$
- $PV(CF_3) = 30,000 / (1.25)^3 = \$15,360.00$
Sum of PV of Inflows = $16,000 + 16,000 + 15,360 = \$47,360$.
$\text{NPV at 25\%} = 47,360 - 50,000 = -\$2,640$.

Interpolate between 10% and 25%:
$IRR = LDR + \left[ \frac{NPV_{LDR}}{NPV_{LDR} - NPV_{HDR}} \right] \times (HDR - LDR)$
$IRR = 10\% + \left[ \frac{11,382.42}{11,382.42 - (-2,640)} \right] \times (25\% - 10\%)$
$IRR = 10\% + \left[ \frac{11,382.42}{14,022.42} \right] \times 15\% = 10\% + 0.8117 \times 15\% \approx 10\% + 12.18\% = 22.18\%$.
The Internal Rate of Return is approximately 22.18%.

6.5 Modified Internal Rate of Return (MIRR)

The traditional IRR assumes that cash inflows are reinvested at the project's own IRR. This can lead to highly unrealistic returns for excellent projects (e.g., assuming a project with 80% IRR can reinvest cash at 80%). The **MIRR** solves this by assuming cash inflows are reinvested at the firm's **cost of capital ($k$)**:

  • MIRR Formula:
    $$PV(\text{Outflows}) = \frac{\text{Terminal Value of Inflows } (TV)}{(1 + MIRR)^n}$$ Where:
    $$TV = \sum_{t=1}^n CF_t \cdot (1 + k)^{n-t}$$ $$PV(\text{Outflows}) = CF_0$$
  • Advantages:
    1. More realistic reinvestment assumption (cost of capital).
    2. Resolves the **Multiple IRR problem** (occurs when a project has non-normal cash flows, i.e., cash flows change signs more than once).

6.6 NPV Profiles and Crossover Rates

An **NPV Profile** is a graph that plots a project's NPV on the y-axis against various discount rates on the x-axis: 

  NPV ($)
    ▲
 50 │ \
    │  \   Project A Profile
    │   \
  0 ┼────\─────o──────────► Discount Rate (%)
    │     \   crossover rate
-10 │      \
  • IRR on the Profile: The point where the NPV curve crosses the x-axis ($NPV = 0$) represents the IRR of the project.
  • Crossover Rate: The discount rate at which the NPV profiles of two mutually exclusive projects intersect, making their NPVs equal.
    • Calculation: Subtracted the cash flows of Project B from Project A year-by-year, and calculate the IRR of the difference cash flow stream.
    • If the cost of capital is less than the crossover rate, an NPV vs. IRR conflict occurs. If it is greater, both NPV and IRR select the same project.

6.7 Accounting Rate of Return and Post-Audit

1. Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)

A traditional non-discounting method that measures the profitability of a project from an accounting perspective:

$$ARR = \frac{\text{Average Net Profit}}{\text{Average Investment}}$$ Where:
$$\text{Average Investment} = \frac{\text{Initial Cost} + \text{Salvage Value}}{2}$$
Pros: Simple to compute, uses readily available accounting statements.
Cons: Completely ignores the **Time Value of Money** and focuses on profits rather than cash flows.

2. The Post-Audit Phase

The capital budgeting process does not end with project selection. The **Post-Audit** is a critical final step:

  • Definition: Comparing the actual cash flows and performance of an active project against the initial forecasts used during the selection phase.
  • Purposes:
    1. Accountability: Discourages managers from inflating forecast revenues to get projects approved.
    2. Improvement: Helps identification of forecasting errors and systemic bias, improving future evaluations.

6.8 Capital Rationing Decisions

In a perfect world, a firm would accept all independent projects that have an NPV > 0. However, firms often face a **Capital Budget Constraint** where they have limited funds and must select only a subset of positive-NPV projects. This is called **Capital Rationing**:

  • Hard Capital Rationing: Imposed by external financial markets (e.g., banks refuse to lend additional money, or equity markets are locked).
  • Soft Capital Rationing: Imposed internally by the firm's senior management (e.g., setting a maximum departmental capital budget of $500,000 to maintain operational control).
  • Optimal Selection Method:
    • Divisible Projects: Rank all projects by their **Profitability Index (PI)** and select the highest-ranked projects until the budget is exhausted.
    • Indivisible Projects: Evaluate all possible combinations of projects that fit within the budget limit and select the combination that yields the **highest total NPV**.

Indivisible Project Selection Worked Example

Problem: An IT firm has a maximum capital budget of $100,000. It is evaluating four independent projects:
- Project W: Cost = $40,000; NPV = $12,000
- Project X: Cost = $50,000; NPV = $15,000
- Project Y: Cost = $60,000; NPV = $20,000
- Project Z: Cost = $30,000; NPV = $8,000
Identify the optimal combination of projects under the budget constraint.

Solution:
Evaluate all feasible combinations whose combined cost $\le \$100,000$:

Combination 1: W + X
- Total Cost = $40,000 + 50,000 = $90,000 (Feasible)
- Total NPV = $12,000 + 15,000 = **$27,000**

Combination 2: W + Y
- Total Cost = $40,000 + 60,000 = $100,000 (Feasible)
- Total NPV = $12,000 + 20,000 = **$32,000**

Combination 3: X + Z
- Total Cost = $50,000 + 30,000 = $80,000 (Feasible)
- Total NPV = $15,000 + 8,000 = **$23,000**

Combination 4: Y + Z
- Total Cost = $60,000 + 30,000 = $90,000 (Feasible)
- Total NPV = $20,000 + 8,000 = **$28,000**

Combination 5: W + X + Z
- Total Cost = $40,000 + 50,000 + 30,000 = $120,000 (Exceeds Budget!)

Conclusion: The optimal combination is **Project W and Project Y**, which fully utilizes the $100,000 budget and maximizes cumulative shareholder wealth with a total NPV of $32,000.

6.9 Cash Flow Estimation in Project Evaluation

The most challenging part of capital budgeting is estimating the relevant project cash flows. These cash flows are estimated across three phases:

  1. Net Initial Investment Outlay ($CF_0$): The net cash outlay required to initiate the project:
    $$\text{Initial Outlay} = \text{Purchase Price} + \text{Installation/Shipping Costs} + \Delta \text{Net Working Capital} - \text{After-tax Salvage of old asset}$$
  2. Operating Cash Flows ($CF_t$): The incremental cash flows generated during the operating life of the project:
    $$CF_t = (\text{Revenues} - \text{Operating Expenses} - \text{Depreciation}) \times (1 - T) + \text{Depreciation}$$ $$CF_t = \text{EBIT}(1 - T) + \text{Depreciation}$$
  3. Terminal Cash Flow: The final net cash flow at the end of the project's life (includes salvage value of the project assets, adjusting for taxes, and recovery of the net operating working capital).

6.10 Capital Budgeting Methods Comparison

Here is a comparison of all the major evaluation techniques:

Method Considers TVM? Considers All Cash Flows? Primary Advantage Primary Disadvantage
Payback Period (PBP) No No (ignores post-payback cash flows) Simple, measures liquidity and risk directly. Biased against long-term projects; ignores TVM.
Accounting Return (ARR) No Yes Uses easy accounting statement data. Focuses on accrual profits, not cash; ignores TVM.
Net Present Value (NPV) Yes Yes Directly measures shareholder wealth addition. Requires accurate cost of capital (WACC) estimation.
Internal Rate (IRR) Yes Yes Expressed as a percentage return (preferred by managers). Can have multiple IRRs; unrealistic reinvestment rate.
Profitability Index (PI) Yes Yes Excellent for capital rationing project ranking. Cannot be used to choose between mutually exclusive projects of different sizes.

6.11 Worked Case Study: Replacement Project Analysis

Replacement decisions are more complex because we must look at **incremental** changes in cash flows:

Replacement Case Study Calculation

Problem: Nepal Software Corp is considering replacing a server stack with a modern cloud-hybrid node:
- **Old Server:** Book value = $20,000. It can be sold today for $8,000.
- **New Server:** Purchase price = $90,000. Installation costs = $5,000. It requires a net operating working capital increase of $5,000.
- **Operating Impact:** The new server reduces annual operating costs by $35,000 (pre-tax).
- **Depreciation:** The new server will be depreciated straight-line to zero over 5 years ($19,000/yr). The old server's remaining straight-line depreciation is $4,000/yr for 5 years.
- **Tax rate** $T = 30\%$.
Calculate: (1) The net initial outlay ($CF_0$) today, and (2) The annual incremental operating cash flow ($CF_1$ to $CF_5$).

Solution:

1. Calculate Net Initial Outlay ($CF_0$):
- Cost of New Server = -$90,000
- New Server Installation = -$5,000
- Sale of Old Server = +$8,000 (Cash Inflow)
- Tax effect on sale of old server:
Loss on sale = Book Value - Sale Price = $20,000 - 8,000 = $12,000.
Tax savings from loss (Tax Shield) = $\text{Loss} \times T = 12,000 \times 0.30 = +\$3,600$ (Cash Inflow).
- Increase in Net Working Capital = -$5,000

$$\text{Net Initial Outlay } CF_0 = -90,000 - 5,000 + 8,000 + 3,600 - 5,000 = -\$88,400$$
The net initial cash outflow today is **$88,400**.

2. Calculate Annual Incremental Operating Cash Flows ($CF_t$):
- Incremental Pre-tax Cost Savings = +$35,000
- Incremental Depreciation Expense:
New Depreciation = $19,000
Old Depreciation = $4,000
$\Delta \text{Depreciation} = 19,000 - 4,000 = \$15,000$.
- Calculate Incremental EBIT:
$\Delta EBIT = \text{Savings} - \Delta \text{Depreciation} = 35,000 - 15,000 = \$20,000$.
- Calculate NOPAT:
$\Delta NOPAT = \Delta EBIT \times (1 - T) = 20,000 \times (1 - 0.30) = \$14,000$.
- Add back incremental depreciation:
$\text{Incremental } CF_t = \Delta NOPAT + \Delta \text{Depreciation} = 14,000 + 15,000 = \$29,000$.
The annual incremental operating cash inflow for years 1 to 5 is **$29,000**.

6.12 Terminal Cash Flow Calculations

At the end of a project's life (Year $n$), the firm recovers cash from winding down operations:
$$\text{Terminal Cash Flow} = \text{Salvage Value of Project Assets} - \text{Taxes on Salvage} + \text{Recovery of Net Working Capital}$$ Taxes on Salvage are calculated as:
$$\text{Taxes on Salvage} = (\text{Salvage Price} - \text{Book Value at Year } n) \times T$$ If the salvage price is greater than the book value, the firm pays tax on the gain (cash outflow); if less, it claims a tax saving (cash inflow).

Terminal Cash Flow Worked Calculation

Problem: For the server stack replacement project in Section 6.11, assume the new server is sold at Year 5 for $15,000. The server was depreciated to a book value of $0. The firm recovers the initial $5,000 net working capital. Tax rate $T = 30\%$. Calculate the terminal cash flow at Year 5.

Solution:
1. Calculate tax on salvage:
Gain on sale = Salvage Price - Book Value = $15,000 - 0 = $15,000.
Tax on gain = $15,000 \times 30\% = \$4,500$ (Cash Outflow).

2. Compute Terminal Cash Flow:
$$\text{Terminal Cash Flow} = \text{Salvage Price} - \text{Tax} + \text{Recovery of Working Capital}$$
$$\text{Terminal Cash Flow} = 15,000 - 4,500 + 5,000 = \$15,500$$
In Year 5, the firm receives an additional cash inflow of **$15,500** from salvage and working capital recovery.

6.13 Capital Budgeting Risk Analysis: Sensitivity vs. Scenario

Since cash flow forecasts are projections of the future, they are subject to forecasting errors. Firms use risk analysis tools to test project robustness:

  • Sensitivity Analysis: Measures the change in NPV in response to a change in a single input variable (e.g., unit sales, selling price, variable cost, or WACC) while holding all other variables constant.
    Benefit: Pinpoints the most critical variables that management must monitor closely.
  • Scenario Analysis: Evaluates the impact on NPV when multiple input variables are changed simultaneously to represent distinct economic states:
    • Best Case: High sales, high unit price, low costs.
    • Base Case: Most likely values for all variables.
    • Worst Case: Low sales, price war, high operating costs.
    Allows managers to calculate the standard deviation and coefficient of variation of the project's NPV.

Unit 10: Risk Management [2 Hrs.]

10.1 Software Risk

What is Risk?

In the context of software engineering, risk is a potential problem — it might happen, it might not. But regardless of the outcome, it is a really good idea to identify it, assess its probability of occurrence, estimate its impact, and establish a contingency plan should the problem actually occur.

According to Robert Charette, risk is characterized by:

  • Uncertainty: The event characterizing the risk may or may not happen; there are no 100% probable risks (those are constraints or facts).
  • Loss: If the risk becomes a reality, unwanted consequences or losses will occur. The losses can be in terms of time, money, quality, or reputation.

Categories of Software Risks

1. Project Risks

Threaten the project plan — if they become real, it is likely that the project schedule will slip and costs will increase:

  • Budget, schedule, and resource risks
  • Staff turnover and availability
  • Scope changes and requirements volatility
  • Organizational and political risks
  • Contractual and procurement risks

2. Technical Risks

Threaten the quality and timeliness of the software to be produced:

  • Design, implementation, interface, and verification risks
  • Technology is new and unproven
  • Requirements are ambiguous or incomplete
  • Integration complexity is underestimated
  • Performance requirements may be difficult to meet
  • Maintenance difficulty due to poor design

3. Business Risks

Threaten the viability of the software to be built:

  • Market risk: Building a product that nobody wants
  • Strategic risk: Product doesn't fit the company's business strategy
  • Sales risk: Product cannot be sold or marketed effectively
  • Management risk: Loss of senior management support
  • Budget risk: Loss of budget or financial commitment

Known Risks vs. Predictable Risks vs. Unpredictable Risks

Risk Type Description Example Handling
Known Risks Can be uncovered after careful evaluation of the project plan and environment Unrealistic delivery date, lack of documented requirements Direct planning and mitigation
Predictable Risks Extrapolated from past project experience Staff turnover, poor communication with customer Historical data analysis, contingency planning
Unpredictable Risks Very difficult to identify in advance Natural disaster, pandemic, sudden regulation change General contingency reserve, insurance

Top 10 Software Risks (Barry Boehm)

  1. Personnel shortfalls
  2. Unrealistic schedules and budgets
  3. Developing the wrong functions and properties
  4. Developing the wrong user interface
  5. Gold plating (adding unnecessary features)
  6. Continuing stream of requirements changes
  7. Shortfalls in externally furnished components or tasks
  8. Shortfalls in externally performed tasks
  9. Real-time performance shortfalls
  10. Straining computer science capabilities

10.2 Risk Identification, Projection and Refinement

Risk Identification

Risk identification is a systematic attempt to specify threats to the project plan. By identifying known and predictable risks, the project manager takes a first step toward avoiding them when possible and controlling them when necessary.

Risk Identification Techniques

1. Checklist-Based Identification

Use a checklist of common software project risks organized by categories:

  • Product size risks: Is the estimated size of the product realistic? What is the confidence level of the size estimate?
  • Business impact risks: How important is this product to the business? What are the consequences of failure?
  • Customer characteristics risks: Is the customer willing to participate? Is the customer technically sophisticated?
  • Process definition risks: Has the organization defined a software process? Is it being followed?
  • Development environment risks: Are the tools available? Are they adequate?
  • Technology risks: Is the technology new to the organization? Is it proven?
  • Staff size and experience risks: Are people available? Do they have the right skills?

2. Brainstorming

  • Team members identify risks in an open forum
  • All risks are recorded without judgment initially
  • Risks are then categorized, evaluated, and prioritized

3. Expert Judgment (Delphi Technique)

  • Multiple experts independently identify risks
  • Results are compiled and shared anonymously
  • Process repeats until consensus is reached

4. SWOT Analysis

  • Strengths: Internal advantages (experienced team, proven technology)
  • Weaknesses: Internal vulnerabilities (skill gaps, tight budget)
  • Opportunities: External factors that could benefit the project
  • Threats: External factors that could harm the project

5. Root Cause Analysis

  • Look at past project failures and identify root causes
  • Proactively identify if similar root causes exist in the current project

Risk Components and Drivers

Each identified risk should be described in terms of four components:

  • Performance risk: Degree of uncertainty that the product will meet its requirements and be fit for its intended use
  • Cost risk: Degree of uncertainty that the project budget will be maintained
  • Support risk: Degree of uncertainty that the resultant software will be easy to correct, adapt, and enhance
  • Schedule risk: Degree of uncertainty that the project schedule will be maintained and that the product will be delivered on time

10.3 Risk Projection (Risk Estimation)

What is Risk Projection?

Risk projection (also called risk estimation) attempts to rate each risk in two ways: (1) the probability that the risk will become reality, and (2) the consequences or impact if the risk does become reality.

Risk Exposure (RE)

  Risk Exposure = Probability of Occurrence × Impact (Loss)

  RE = P × L

  Where:
    P = Probability of the risk occurring (0 to 1)
    L = Loss or impact if the risk occurs (monetary or other measure)

10.4 Risk Refinement

What is Risk Refinement?

During early stages of project planning, a risk may be stated in a general way. As time passes and more is learned about the project and the risk, it may be possible to refine the risk into a set of more detailed, more specific risks.

CTC (Condition-Transition-Consequence) Format

One way to refine a risk is to represent it using the CTC format: 

  Given that [condition],
  there is concern that [transition/event],
  which would lead to [consequence].

  Example:
  General Risk: "Staff turnover might affect the project"

  Refined Risks:
  R1: Given that only one developer knows the payment module,
      there is concern that this developer might leave,
      which would lead to 3-month delay in payment feature delivery.

  R2: Given that the team has no backup for the DevOps engineer,
      there is concern that the DevOps engineer might become unavailable,
      which would lead to inability to deploy to production.

  R3: Given that two team members have received competing offers,
      there is concern that they might resign simultaneously,
      which would lead to loss of 40% of the development capacity.

10.5 Risk Analysis Management and Process

Risk Management Process

The risk management process is a continuous activity that occurs throughout the software project lifecycle:

Risk Management Strategies

1. Risk Avoidance

Changing the project plan to eliminate the risk or condition, or to protect the project objectives from its impact:

  • Choose proven technology over cutting-edge technology
  • Reduce scope to avoid complex features
  • Hire experienced staff instead of training newcomers

2. Risk Mitigation

Taking actions to reduce the probability of occurrence or the impact:

  • Cross-train team members to reduce key-person dependency
  • Build prototypes to reduce technical uncertainty
  • Use incremental delivery to get early customer feedback
  • Implement quality assurance processes to reduce defects

3. Risk Transfer

Shifting the impact of a risk to a third party:

  • Outsource risky components to experienced vendors
  • Purchase insurance for catastrophic risks
  • Use warranties and service level agreements (SLAs)

4. Risk Acceptance

Acknowledging the risk and choosing not to take action unless the risk occurs:

  • Active acceptance: Develop a contingency plan (Plan B)
  • Passive acceptance: Accept the consequences if the risk occurs; appropriate only for low-impact, low-probability risks

Risk Monitoring

Risk monitoring is the ongoing tracking of identified risks, identifying new risks, and evaluating the effectiveness of risk management strategies:

  • Regular risk review meetings (weekly or bi-weekly)
  • Update risk register with new information
  • Track risk indicators (early warning signs)
  • Reassess probability and impact as the project progresses
  • Close risks that are no longer applicable
  • Identify new risks that emerge during the project

10.6 The RMMM Plan

What is an RMMM Plan?

The RMMM (Risk Mitigation, Monitoring, and Management) Plan is a document that describes the risk management strategy for a software project. It is part of the Software Project Plan and documents how risks will be handled throughout the project lifecycle.

Components of an RMMM Plan

1. Risk Identification Summary

  • List of all identified risks
  • Risk categories (project, technical, business)
  • Risk descriptions and context

2. Risk Analysis Summary

  • Probability assessment for each risk
  • Impact assessment for each risk
  • Risk exposure calculations
  • Priority ranking

3. Mitigation Strategy

  • Specific actions to reduce probability or impact of each high-priority risk
  • Responsible person for each mitigation action
  • Timeline for implementing mitigation actions
  • Resources required for mitigation

4. Monitoring Strategy

  • Risk indicators (measurable factors that signal risk is becoming reality)
  • Monitoring frequency and methods
  • Trigger conditions for escalation
  • Reporting mechanisms

5. Contingency Plan

  • Actions to take if the risk becomes a reality
  • Fallback positions and alternative approaches
  • Emergency procedures and escalation paths

RMMM Plan Example

Risk ID R001
Risk Key developer (lead backend engineer) leaves the project
Category Project Risk — Personnel
Probability 0.30 (30%)
Impact High — 3-month delay, $50K additional cost
Risk Exposure $15,000
Mitigation 1. Cross-train at least two other developers on backend systems (by Week 4)
2. Document all critical system architecture and design decisions
3. Implement pair programming on critical modules
4. Ensure competitive compensation and engagement
Monitoring Indicators: Employee satisfaction surveys, 1-on-1 meeting feedback, job market activity
Frequency: Monthly monitoring
Trigger: Developer expresses dissatisfaction or receives outside offers
Contingency 1. Immediately assign cross-trained developer to lead backend
2. Engage contractor for temporary support (pre-identified vendor list)
3. Adjust schedule by 4 weeks for knowledge transfer period
4. Escalate to management for resource reallocation
Owner Project Manager
Status Active — Monitoring

Risk Register

A risk register (or risk log) is a living document that captures all identified risks along with their analysis, mitigation strategies, owners, and current status. It is updated throughout the project lifecycle.

Summary: Risk management is a proactive approach to identifying, analyzing, and responding to potential problems before they become crises. The RMMM plan provides a structured framework for mitigation (reducing probability/impact), monitoring (tracking indicators), and management (contingency planning). Effective risk management significantly increases the probability of project success by preventing small issues from becoming project-ending catastrophes.
